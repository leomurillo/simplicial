## Abstract

The ordinary complex plane contains a regular 2-simplex in plain sight: the three cube roots of unity

$$
1,\qquad p=e^{2\pi i/3},\qquad \ell=p^2=e^{4\pi i/3},
$$

which satisfy \(1+p+\ell=0\).  This note rewrites the differential core of complex analysis in that equilateral frame.  For a smooth complex-valued function \(f\), let \(D_1 f,D_p f,D_\ell f\) denote directional derivatives along the three unit directions \(1,p,\ell\).  These three derivatives are linearly dependent,

$$
D_1+D_p+D_\ell=0,
$$

as differential operators.  Their two nontrivial Fourier-weighted combinations recover the Wirtinger derivatives:

$$
\partial_z=\frac13(D_1+\ell D_p+pD_\ell),
\qquad
\partial_{\bar z}=\frac13(D_1+pD_p+\ell D_\ell).
$$

Consequently holomorphicity is equivalent to vanishing of the antiholomorphic cyclic mode, i.e. to the triadic Cauchy-Riemann equation

$$
D_1 f+pD_p f+\ell D_\ell f=0.
$$

The same frame gives a symmetric Laplacian identity,

$$
D_1^2+D_p^2+D_\ell^2=\frac32\Delta.
$$

Thus the first-order Cauchy-Riemann operator and the second-order Laplace operator can both be expressed without choosing rectangular axes.  The construction is not a new function theory for \(N=3\): it is ordinary complex analysis written in the zero-sum equilateral simplicial frame.  Its value is structural.  It makes the \(A_2\) simplex, the cube roots of unity, and the triangular-lattice stencil native rather than auxiliary.  The final sections situate this \(N=3\) result as the first planar rung of a cautious cyclotomic-simplicial ladder, parallel in spirit but not in algebraic content to the Cayley-Dickson sequence.

**Keywords:** complex analysis, Cauchy-Riemann equations, Wirtinger derivatives, cube roots of unity, Eisenstein integers, equiangular tight frames, regular simplex, triangular lattice, discrete complex analysis, cyclotomic symmetry, zero-sum coordinates.

## 1. Introduction

The standard presentation of complex analysis uses the rectangular frame \(1,i\).  This choice is natural for Cartesian coordinates, but it hides a second elementary symmetry of the complex plane: the equilateral triple

$$
E=\{1,p,\ell\},\qquad p=e^{2\pi i/3},\qquad \ell=p^2=\bar p.
$$

The defining algebraic relations are

$$
p^3=1,\qquad p^2=\ell,\qquad p\ell=1,\qquad 1+p+\ell=0.
$$

The last identity is the zero-sum relation of a regular simplex.  Viewed as real unit vectors in \(\mathbb R^2\), the three roots \(1,p,\ell\) have pairwise inner product \(-1/2\), and therefore form the regular simplex equiangular tight frame (ETF) for the plane.  In this sense the complex plane has two elementary coordinate stories:

1. the square story, built from \(1\) and \(i\), where \(i^2=-1\);
2. the triangular story, built from \(1,p,\ell\), where \(p^2+p+1=0\).

This note develops the second story only as far as the first unavoidable test: can the Cauchy-Riemann equations and the Laplacian be written cleanly in the equilateral frame?

The answer is yes.  The three directional derivatives along \(1,p,\ell\) decompose into three Fourier modes.  One mode vanishes identically because \(1+p+\ell=0\).  The other two modes are exactly \(\partial_z\) and \(\partial_{\bar z}\), up to the common factor \(3\).  Holomorphicity is therefore the vanishing of one nontrivial cyclic mode.

This is a modest result, but it is the right modest result.  Once the frame is chosen, the formulas are immediate from \(C_3\)-character theory.  The contribution is therefore structural bookkeeping: the \(N=3\) simplicial/cyclotomic frame does not fight complex analysis; it diagonalizes its first-order structure.

### 1.1 What This Paper Claims

The paper claims:

1. The cube-root frame is the \(N=3\) zero-sum simplicial frame in the complex plane.
2. Directional derivatives along the three frame directions admit a cyclic Fourier decomposition.
3. The two nontrivial cyclic modes are the Wirtinger derivatives \(\partial_z\) and \(\partial_{\bar z}\).
4. The Laplacian is the normalized sum of second directional derivatives along the three frame directions.
5. The corresponding central-difference formulas give a natural triangular-lattice approximation to \(\partial_z\), \(\partial_{\bar z}\), and \(\Delta\).

### 1.2 What This Paper Does Not Claim

For \(N=3\), the construction is not a new algebra beyond \(\mathbb C\).  The equilateral frame is a different presentation of the same plane.  In particular:

- the cube roots of unity already live in \(\mathbb C\);
- holomorphicity remains the ordinary condition \(\partial_{\bar z}f=0\);
- the finite-difference section is a local stencil, not a full replacement for established discrete complex analysis on rhombic, isoradial, or quad-graph structures.

The point is not novelty by escaping complex analysis.  The point is symmetry bookkeeping: the rectangular frame makes \(i^2=-1\) primary, while the equilateral frame makes \(1+p+\ell=0\) primary.

### 1.3 Contribution Scale

This note is best read as a companion note to the simplicial-coordinate program, not as a claim of a new complex function theory.  The main observation is that the Wirtinger pair is exactly the pair of nontrivial \(C_3\)-Fourier modes of the directional-derivative system.  The same decomposition gives the six-neighbor triangular Laplacian.  These identities are elementary; their purpose here is to make the \(A_2\) simplex and the cube-root frame primary enough that later \(N=5,7,\ldots\) experiments have a calibrated base case.

## 2. The Equilateral Simplicial Frame

Let

$$
p=-\frac12+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i,\qquad
\ell=-\frac12-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i.
$$

Then

$$
p^2=\ell,\qquad \ell^2=p,\qquad p\ell=1,\qquad 1+p+\ell=0.
$$

As real vectors, \(1,p,\ell\) are unit vectors separated by \(120^\circ\).  Their real inner products are

$$
\operatorname{Re}(1\bar p)=\operatorname{Re}(p\bar\ell)=\operatorname{Re}(\ell\bar 1)=-\frac12.
$$

Thus they are the vertices of a centered equilateral triangle and form a regular simplex ETF for \(\mathbb R^2\).

### 2.1 Coordinates and Gauge

Define the synthesis map

$$
S:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb C,\qquad S(a,b,c)=a+bp+c\ell.
$$

Since \(1+p+\ell=0\), the kernel of \(S\) is the gauge line

$$
\ker S=\mathbb R(1,1,1).
$$

The canonical representative of a coordinate class is the zero-sum hyperplane

$$
H_3=\{(a,b,c)\in\mathbb R^3:a+b+c=0\}.
$$

On \(H_3\), \(S\) is an isomorphism from the zero-sum plane to \(\mathbb C\).  The Gram matrix of the frame is

$$
G=
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & -1/2 & -1/2\\
-1/2 & 1 & -1/2\\
-1/2 & -1/2 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
=\frac32 I-\frac12 J,
$$

where \(J\) is the all-ones matrix.  For \(x=(a,b,c)\),

$$
|Sx|^2=x^\top Gx.
$$

If \(x\in H_3\), then

$$
|Sx|^2=\frac32(a^2+b^2+c^2).
$$

This is the \(N=3\) instance of the regular-simplex metric identity.

## 3. Directional Derivatives in the Triadic Frame

Let \(f:U\subset\mathbb C\to\mathbb C\) be \(C^1\).  For a unit complex number \(\alpha\), define the directional derivative

$$
D_\alpha f(z)=\left.\frac{d}{dt}f(z+t\alpha)\right|_{t=0},
\qquad t\in\mathbb R.
$$

The ordinary Wirtinger derivatives are

$$
\partial_z=\frac12(\partial_x-i\partial_y),
\qquad
\partial_{\bar z}=\frac12(\partial_x+i\partial_y).
$$

### Lemma 3.1

For every unit complex number \(\alpha\),

$$
D_\alpha=\alpha\,\partial_z+\bar\alpha\,\partial_{\bar z}.
$$

**Proof.**  Write \(\alpha=e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\).  Then

$$
D_\alpha=\cos\theta\,\partial_x+\sin\theta\,\partial_y.
$$

Using

$$
\partial_x=\partial_z+\partial_{\bar z},
\qquad
\partial_y=i(\partial_z-\partial_{\bar z}),
$$

we get

$$
D_\alpha=(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\partial_z
+(\cos\theta-i\sin\theta)\partial_{\bar z}
=\alpha\partial_z+\bar\alpha\partial_{\bar z}.
$$

\(\square\)

### 3.1 The Three Cyclic Modes

Define three operators:

$$
\mathcal D_0:=D_1+D_p+D_\ell,
$$

$$
\mathcal D_z:=D_1+\ell D_p+pD_\ell,
$$

$$
\mathcal D_{\bar z}:=D_1+pD_p+\ell D_\ell.
$$

These are the three Fourier-weighted sums over the cyclic group \(C_3\).  The weights \(1,1,1\) give the trivial character, while \(1,\ell,p\) and \(1,p,\ell\) give the two nontrivial characters.

The labels are intentionally tied to the output mode rather than to the apparent weights.  The weights are dual characters acting on covectors: \(D_1+\ell D_p+pD_\ell\) projects onto the \(z\)-mode, while \(D_1+pD_p+\ell D_\ell\) projects onto the \(\bar z\)-mode.  This conjugation is the same bookkeeping that makes Fourier coefficients pair a vector mode with the inverse character.

### Theorem 3.2

The triadic derivative operators satisfy

$$
\mathcal D_0=0,\qquad
\mathcal D_z=3\partial_z,\qquad
\mathcal D_{\bar z}=3\partial_{\bar z}.
$$

**Proof.**  By Lemma 3.1,

$$
D_1=\partial_z+\partial_{\bar z},
$$

$$
D_p=p\partial_z+\ell\partial_{\bar z},
$$

$$
D_\ell=\ell\partial_z+p\partial_{\bar z}.
$$

For the trivial sum,

$$
D_1+D_p+D_\ell=(1+p+\ell)\partial_z+(1+\ell+p)\partial_{\bar z}=0.
$$

For \(\mathcal D_z\),

$$
D_1+\ell D_p+pD_\ell
=(1+\ell p+p\ell)\partial_z+(1+\ell^2+p^2)\partial_{\bar z}.
$$

Since \(\ell p=p\ell=1\) and \(\ell^2+p^2=p+\ell=-1\), this gives

$$
\mathcal D_z=3\partial_z.
$$

Similarly,

$$
D_1+pD_p+\ell D_\ell
=(1+p^2+\ell^2)\partial_z+(1+p\ell+\ell p)\partial_{\bar z}
=3\partial_{\bar z}.
$$

\(\square\)

## 4. Triadic Cauchy-Riemann Equations

The ordinary Cauchy-Riemann condition is

$$
\partial_{\bar z}f=0.
$$

By Theorem 3.2, this is equivalent to the triadic condition

$$
\boxed{D_1 f+pD_p f+\ell D_\ell f=0.}
$$

This is the equilateral-frame Cauchy-Riemann equation.

Equivalently, holomorphicity is the vanishing of the antiholomorphic cyclic mode \(\mathcal D_{\bar z}\).  The complementary mode \(\mathcal D_z=3\partial_z\) does not vanish in general; it encodes the complex derivative itself.

### 4.1 Real Form

Let \(f=u+iv\).  Since

$$
D_p=-\frac12\partial_x+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\partial_y,
\qquad
D_\ell=-\frac12\partial_x-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\partial_y,
$$

we have

$$
D_1+pD_p+\ell D_\ell
=\frac32(\partial_x+i\partial_y)
=3\partial_{\bar z}.
$$

Thus

$$
(D_1+pD_p+\ell D_\ell)f=0
$$

is equivalent to

$$
u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x.
$$

The usual rectangular Cauchy-Riemann equations are recovered, but the equilateral frame reaches them through a cyclic zero-sum identity.

### 4.2 Examples

For \(f(z)=z\),

$$
D_1f=1,\qquad D_pf=p,\qquad D_\ell f=\ell.
$$

Then

$$
D_1f+pD_pf+\ell D_\ell f
=1+p^2+\ell^2
=1+\ell+p
=0.
$$

For \(f(z)=\bar z\),

$$
D_1f=1,\qquad D_pf=\ell,\qquad D_\ell f=p.
$$

Then

$$
D_1f+pD_pf+\ell D_\ell f
=1+p\ell+\ell p
=3.
$$

So \(z\) passes the triadic Cauchy-Riemann test and \(\bar z\) fails it exactly as expected.

## 5. The Symmetric Laplacian

The three second directional derivatives also combine cleanly.

### Theorem 5.1

For every \(C^2\) function,

$$
D_1^2+D_p^2+D_\ell^2=\frac32\Delta,
$$

where

$$
\Delta=\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2=4\partial_z\partial_{\bar z}.
$$

**Proof.**  From Lemma 3.1,

$$
D_\alpha^2
=\alpha^2\partial_z^2+2\alpha\bar\alpha\,\partial_z\partial_{\bar z}
+\bar\alpha^2\partial_{\bar z}^2.
$$

For \(\alpha\in\{1,p,\ell\}\), \(\alpha\bar\alpha=1\).  Also

$$
1+p^2+\ell^2=1+\ell+p=0,
$$

and similarly

$$
1+\bar p^2+\bar \ell^2=0.
$$

Therefore

$$
D_1^2+D_p^2+D_\ell^2
=6\partial_z\partial_{\bar z}
=\frac32\Delta.
$$

\(\square\)

Equivalently,

$$
\Delta=\frac23(D_1^2+D_p^2+D_\ell^2).
$$

Since

$$
\mathcal D_z\mathcal D_{\bar z}=9\partial_z\partial_{\bar z},
$$

we also have

$$
D_1^2+D_p^2+D_\ell^2=\frac23\mathcal D_z\mathcal D_{\bar z}.
$$

Thus the equilateral Laplacian is the product of the two nontrivial first-order cyclic modes, up to scale.

## 6. A Triangular-Lattice Stencil

The continuum formulas above suggest a simple central-difference discretization on the triangular lattice

$$
\Lambda_h=h(\mathbb Z+p\mathbb Z).
$$

This is the scaled Eisenstein lattice \(h\mathbb Z[p]\).  Its algebraic norm is

$$
|a+bp|^2=a^2-ab+b^2,
$$

so the same cube-root arithmetic that drives the continuum identities also supplies the natural lattice carrier.

The six nearest-neighbor directions are

$$
\pm 1,\qquad \pm p,\qquad \pm \ell.
$$

For \(\alpha\in\{1,p,\ell\}\), define the central directional difference

$$
\delta_\alpha^h f(z)=\frac{f(z+h\alpha)-f(z-h\alpha)}{2h}.
$$

Then the triadic discrete Wirtinger stencils are

$$
\partial_z^{(h)}f
:=\frac13(\delta_1^h+\ell\delta_p^h+p\delta_\ell^h)f,
$$

$$
\partial_{\bar z}^{(h)}f
:=\frac13(\delta_1^h+p\delta_p^h+\ell\delta_\ell^h)f.
$$

For smooth \(f\),

$$
\partial_z^{(h)}f=\partial_z f+O(h^2),
\qquad
\partial_{\bar z}^{(h)}f=\partial_{\bar z} f+O(h^2).
$$

A local discrete holomorphicity condition is therefore

$$
\boxed{\partial_{\bar z}^{(h)}f(z)=0.}
$$

Likewise, the second-difference triangular Laplacian

$$
\Delta_\triangle^{(h)} f(z)
:=\frac23\sum_{\alpha\in\{1,p,\ell\}}
\frac{f(z+h\alpha)-2f(z)+f(z-h\alpha)}{h^2}
$$

satisfies

$$
\Delta_\triangle^{(h)} f=\Delta f+O(h^2).
$$

This is the normalized six-neighbor triangular-lattice Laplacian.

### 6.1 Relation to Established Discrete Complex Analysis and Numerics

Discrete complex analysis is a mature subject.  Classical lines go through Ferrand, Duffin, Isaacs, Mercat, Bobenko, Suris, Chelkak, Smirnov, Kenyon, and others, with prominent formulations on square, rhombic, quad-graph, and isoradial structures.

The six-neighbor triangular/hexagonal stencil is also standard furniture in numerical analysis and signal processing.  Finite-difference analogues of Poisson and Dirichlet problems were studied classically by Bramble and Hubbard, while the hexagonal sampling tradition in two-dimensional signal processing includes Mersereau's work and its descendants.

The stencil above should be read as a local equilateral-frame discretization of the continuum identities in Sections 3-5.  It is not yet a complete discrete function theory.  To become one, it would need boundary value theory, contour integrals, convergence theorems, and compatibility with a chosen graph/dual-graph structure.  The natural next comparison is with isoradial graph formulations, because the triangular and hexagonal lattices are isoradial and already carry \(60^\circ/120^\circ\) geometry.

## 7. Relation to the Cyclotomic-Simplicial Ladder

The \(N=3\) case is special because the zero-sum hyperplane \(H_3\) is two-dimensional.  The nontrivial cyclic modes of \(C_3\) are exactly the two Wirtinger directions.  This makes \(N=3\) the only prime simplicial case where the entire nontrivial cyclotomic representation is already one real plane.

For general \(N\), let \(C_N\) be the cyclic coordinate shift on \(\mathbb R^N\).  It preserves the gauge line \(\mathbb R\mathbf 1\) and the zero-sum hyperplane \(H_N\).  The basic identities are

$$
C_N^N=I,
$$

and on \(H_N\),

$$
I+C_N+C_N^2+\cdots+C_N^{N-1}=0.
$$

The polynomial in the last display is

$$
M_N(x):=\frac{x^N-1}{x-1}=1+x+\cdots+x^{N-1}.
$$

For composite \(N\), \(M_N(x)\) is not the cyclotomic polynomial \(\Phi_N(x)\); rather,

$$
M_N(x)=\prod_{\substack{d\mid N\\d>1}}\Phi_d(x).
$$

This factorization is the algebraic reason composite cyclic systems split into lower cyclotomic pieces.  For prime \(N=q\), however,

$$
\Phi_q(C_q)=0,\qquad
\Phi_q(x)=1+x+x^2+\cdots+x^{q-1}.
$$

Equivalently, over \(\mathbb Q\),

$$
H_{q,\mathbb Q}:=H_q\cap\mathbb Q^q
$$

is an irreducible \(\mathbb Q[C_q]\)-module and is isomorphic, after choosing a cyclic generator, to the cyclotomic field [Serre; Washington]

$$
\mathbb Q[x]/(\Phi_q(x))\cong\mathbb Q(\zeta_q).
$$

This is the precise sense in which prime \(q\)-simplicial systems are primitive: primitive over the rational cyclic representation, not indecomposable over \(\mathbb R\).

Over \(\mathbb R\), the same representation decomposes into conjugate Fourier pairs.  For odd prime \(q\),

$$
H_q=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{(q-1)/2} V_k,
$$

where each \(V_k\) is a real two-dimensional plane on which \(C_q\) acts by rotation through \(2\pi k/q\).  Thus the prime ladder is rationally irreducible but real-spectrally planar.

### 7.1 Low Prime Rungs

The \(q=2\) case is the sign oscillation.  On

$$
H_2=\{(a,-a):a\in\mathbb R\},
$$

the shift acts as \(-I\), so the law is \(I+C_2=0\).

The \(q=3\) relation is

$$
1+p+p^2=0,
$$

which gives the equilateral complex plane developed in this note.

For \(q=5\), the zero-sum space has real dimension four and decomposes over \(\mathbb R\) into two orthogonal cyclotomic planes, corresponding to the Fourier frequencies \(1\) and \(2\).  The shift rotates these planes through \(2\pi/5\) and \(4\pi/5\).  The golden ratio appears through

$$
\cos(2\pi/5)=\frac{\sqrt5-1}{4},\qquad
\cos(4\pi/5)=-\frac{\sqrt5+1}{4}.
$$

This gives a real connection to pentagonal and icosahedral arithmetic, but the two \(C_5\)-planes are not coupled at the linear level; they form a direct sum.

For \(q=7\), the real decomposition has three rotation planes, through angles \(2\pi/7,4\pi/7,6\pi/7\).  The corresponding cosines lie in the real cubic subfield of \(\mathbb Q(\zeta_7)\).  This is a natural place to compare with seven-dimensional structures such as the octonionic cross product, but no such cross product is determined by the cyclic representation alone.  Any octonionic bridge would require extra \(G_2\), Fano-plane, or orientation data beyond \(C_7\).

### 7.2 Comparison With Cayley-Dickson

The comparison with the Cayley-Dickson sequence should be read as an analogy of organizing principles, not as an isomorphism of constructions; for background on the octonionic side of that ladder, see Baez [Baez].

| Feature | Cayley-Dickson ladder | Cyclotomic-simplicial ladder |
|---|---|---|
| Progression | \(1,2,4,8,\ldots\) real dimensions | \(p-1\) dimensions for prime \(p\) |
| Mechanism | doubling, conjugation, norm composition | cyclic shift, zero-sum quotient, cyclotomic factor |
| First law | \(i^2+1=0\) | \(\Phi_p(C_p)=0\) on \(H_p\) |
| Symmetry exposed | spinorial double covers and composition algebras | abelian characters and Fourier/cyclotomic modes |
| Cost | commutativity/associativity degrade | no division-algebra structure is produced |

The Cayley-Dickson ladder builds algebras.  The cyclotomic-simplicial ladder builds representations.  That distinction is essential.  The prime \(p\) relation \(\Phi_p(C_p)=0\) is a legitimate sibling of \(i^2+1=0\) as a minimal polynomial law, but it does not by itself produce quaternions, octonions, or a normed division algebra.

For prime \(q>3\), this also means that the linear first-order theory is mostly finished by character theory: it is a stack of \((q-1)/2\) independent complex planes.  Any genuinely new prime-\(q\) analytic structure must therefore enter through something that the linear decomposition does not see: multiplication of modes, lattice compatibility, contour/integral theory, or boundary kernels.

## 8. Exponent Lattices and Dimensional Analysis

There is a useful, but limited, analogy between the gauge quotient \(H_N\) and dimensional analysis.  Physical dimensions form a free abelian multiplicative group.  If \(M,L,T\) are base dimensions, then

$$
M^aL^bT^c\longleftrightarrow (a,b,c)\in\mathbb Z^3.
$$

The Buckingham Pi theorem [Buckingham] is kernel computation on such exponent matrices: dimensionless products are integer relations among exponent columns.

Prime factorization gives an analogous exponent-lattice model for positive rationals:

$$
n=\prod_p p^{v_p(n)}
\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
(v_2(n),v_3(n),v_5(n),\ldots).
$$

In both cases, multiplicative structure becomes additive lattice structure after taking exponents or valuations.  This is the safe bridge: dimensional analysis and prime valuation theory share a kernel/nullspace formalism for multiplicative invariants.

The analogy should not be pushed into algorithmic claims about factoring.  A prime valuation matrix presupposes knowledge of the relevant prime valuations.  Its value here is conceptual: it places scaling characters, roots of unity, and gauge quotients under the shared language of characters, exponent lattices, and invariant kernels.

## 9. Low-Hanging Next Problems

This note leaves several deliberately small next problems.

### 9.1 Triadic Cauchy-Pompeiu Formula

The Dirichlet problem on an equilateral triangle is classical, and its symmetry is not in doubt.  A more meaningful target is a Cauchy-Pompeiu or Green-type identity adapted to triangular contours, with

$$
\mathcal D_{\bar z}=D_1+pD_p+\ell D_\ell
$$

as the primary first-order operator.  The question is whether the three-direction formulation yields a compact boundary formula rather than merely a symmetric restatement of a known boundary value problem.

### 9.2 Triadic Cauchy Integral Form

Rewrite \(df=f_z\,dz+f_{\bar z}\,d\bar z\) using the three dual one-forms associated with \(1,p,\ell\).  The goal is to see whether Cauchy's integral theorem has a natural three-direction decomposition around triangular contours.

### 9.3 Discrete Holomorphicity on the Triangular/Hexagonal Pair

Compare

$$
\partial_{\bar z}^{(h)}f=0
$$

with known isoradial and rhombic definitions.  The likely outcome is not a replacement but a translation dictionary between the local triangular stencil and existing quad/dual graph equations.

### 9.4 The \(N=5\) Fourier-Mode Experiment

Repeat the derivative-mode construction for five equally spaced directions.  The expected first-order result is not one pair of Wirtinger derivatives but two independent nontrivial frequency pairs.  The useful question is therefore not whether the linear operator splits; it does.  The useful question is where the split stops being enough: multiplication of functions, simultaneous lattice discretization on \(\mathbb Z[\zeta_5]\), contour kernels in \(H_5\), or boundary value theory.

### 9.5 The \(N=7\) Octonionic Comparison

Compare the three \(C_7\)-rotation planes with the seven-dimensional data needed for the octonionic cross product.  The working hypothesis should be conservative: \(C_7\) supplies cyclic harmonic structure, while octonions require additional incidence/orientation data.  The first useful result would be a precise statement of what extra data are needed to turn the cyclic \(H_7\) picture into a \(G_2\)-compatible one, if such a translation is possible.

## 10. Conclusion

The complex plane can be presented in a rectangular frame \((1,i)\) or in an equilateral simplicial frame \((1,p,\ell)\).  In the rectangular frame, the elementary law is

$$
i^2=-1.
$$

In the equilateral frame, the elementary law is

$$
1+p+\ell=0,
\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
p^2+p+1=0.
$$

The differential calculus respects this second law.  Directional derivatives along \(1,p,\ell\) split into cyclic modes:

$$
D_1+D_p+D_\ell=0,
$$

$$
D_1+\ell D_p+pD_\ell=3\partial_z,
$$

$$
D_1+pD_p+\ell D_\ell=3\partial_{\bar z}.
$$

Holomorphicity is therefore the vanishing of the antiholomorphic cyclic mode,

$$
D_1 f+pD_p f+\ell D_\ell f=0.
$$

The Laplacian is equally symmetric:

$$
D_1^2+D_p^2+D_\ell^2=\frac32\Delta.
$$

This gives a compact first bridge between simplicial coordinates, roots of unity, and the analytic operators of the complex plane.  The construction is ordinary complex analysis seen through the \(A_2\) simplex, but that is precisely why it is a good base case: it shows which parts of the cyclotomic-simplicial program are genuine structure and which parts are coordinate shadow.

Beyond \(N=3\), the cautious slogan is that the Cayley-Dickson ladder builds algebras while the cyclotomic-simplicial ladder builds cyclic representations.  Prime \(q\) gives a rationally irreducible cyclotomic module on \(H_q\), but over \(\mathbb R\) it splits into independent rotation planes.  The open work therefore begins where character theory stops: multiplication, lattice compatibility, contour kernels, and any additional incidence data needed for exceptional comparisons such as \(N=7\) and octonions.
