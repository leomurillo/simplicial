## Abstract

Paper 8 constructs the parity-dual simplex from the unique nonconstant
one-dimensional cyclotomic block of the cyclic simplex representation: the
\(\Phi_2\) line.  This paper extracts the general arithmetic mechanism behind
that construction.  Let \(R=A/I\) be a finite quotient of a Dedekind domain,
equipped with a generating additive character so that \(R\) is identified with
its Pontryagin dual.  The regular simplex

$$
V_R=\{v_a=e_a-|R|^{-1}{\bf 1}:a\in R\}
$$

in the zero-sum hyperplane \(H_R\subset \mathbb C^R\) decomposes according to
the conductors of additive characters.  For each nontrivial divisor
\(\mathfrak d\mid I\), the conductor block

$$
H_R^{[\mathfrak d]}
=
\operatorname{span}\{\chi:\operatorname{cond}(\chi)=\mathfrak d\}
$$

has an orthogonal projector whose kernel is a generalized Ramanujan sum:

$$
(P_{\mathfrak d})_{a,c}
=
|R|^{-1}C_{\mathfrak d}(a-c).
$$

Changing signs independently on the nontrivial conductor blocks produces a
finite block-sign group \(B_R\).  After quotienting by the finite kernel
\(K_R\) of block signs that merely relabel the original simplex, the resulting
spectral compound is a unit-norm tight frame with explicit cross-Gram entries

$$
\langle T_\epsilon v_a,T_\eta v_b\rangle
=
|R|^{-1}
\sum_{\mathfrak d\mid I,\ \mathfrak d\ne 1}
\epsilon_{\mathfrak d}\eta_{\mathfrak d}
C_{\mathfrak d}(a-b).
$$

The integer specialization \(A=\mathbb Z\), \(R=\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z\)
recovers the cyclotomic block-sign compound of Paper 8, with ordinary
Ramanujan sums and \(\Phi_d\)-blocks.  The function-field specialization
\(A=\mathbb F_q[t]\), \(R=A/(f)\) replaces them by Carlitz-Hayes conductor
blocks and polynomial Ramanujan sums

$$
C_d(h)=\sum_{e\mid \gcd(d,h)} |e|\mu(d/e).
$$

The new phenomenon occurs at the meeting point of the coordinate and reflection
layers.  In the integer case, \(\Phi_2\) is the unique nonconstant
one-dimensional block.  In finite Dedekind quotients, one-dimensional
nonconstant conductor blocks occur exactly for squarefree products of norm-two
prime ideals; in the polynomial case, exactly for squarefree products of
distinct linear factors over \(\mathbb F_2\).  The primitive meeting points are
the individual norm-two factors.  Thus the integer \(\Phi_2\) line is not
renamed away: it is the one-prime collapse, or one-factor shadow, of the binary
multi-meeting-point theorem.

Although the first worked split atlas is written as
\(\mathbb F_2[t]/(t(t+1))\), it is not only a function-field example.  The
same quotient occurs in the quadratic number ring
\(\mathcal O_{\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-7})}/(2)\), so the binary multi-meeting-point
phenomenon already appears inside ordinary number-ring arithmetic as soon as
\((2)\) splits.  The first non-collapsed all-binary atlas
\(R\cong\mathbb F_2^3\) is also computed explicitly: it gives a \(7\)-cube
decomposed into sixteen arithmetic simplex images and is realized by a cyclic
cubic number ring with \((2)\) split completely.

**Keywords:** regular simplex, finite quotient ring, Dedekind quotient,
Frobenius ring, additive character, conductor, Ramanujan sum, Hayes sum,
Carlitz module, function field, tight frame, association scheme, parity dual.

## 1. Introduction

The regular simplex in the zero-sum hyperplane is a good detector of arithmetic
decomposition.  In the cyclic integer case, the coordinate labels form
\(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z\), translations are generated by one cyclic shift, and
the nontrivial spectral blocks are the cyclotomic summands indexed by
\(d\mid N\), \(d>1\).  Paper 8 studies the reflections obtained by changing
signs on those blocks.  The simplest reflection is the \(\Phi_2\) sign flip:
when \(N\) is even, \(\Phi_2(X)=X+1\) gives a single parity line in \(H_N\).
Reflecting the simplex across the orthogonal complement of that line produces
the parity-dual simplex.

For a reader coming to this paper first, the integer geometry needed from
Paper 8 is the following compact picture.  Let

$$
v_a=e_a-\frac1N\mathbf 1,\qquad a\in\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z,
$$

be the centered regular simplex in the zero-sum hyperplane \(H_N\).  The
cyclic shift diagonalizes over the nontrivial Fourier characters, and the real
cyclotomic block \(H_N^{[d]}\) is the sum of the character lines whose
primitive conductor is \(d\).  A block-sign operator changes signs on selected
\(H_N^{[d]}\)'s and sends the simplex to another regular simplex image.  Its
cross-Gram entries are normalized Ramanujan sums, so the coordinate colors are
controlled by divisor data.  The exceptional feature of \(d=2\) is that
\(\varphi(2)=1\): the block is a single real line, the sign flip is an honest
codimension-one reflection, and its character values are the even/odd
coordinate bipartition.

The \(\Phi_2\) line is special for two independent reasons.  First, it is a
reflection block: its sign flip is a codimension-one Householder reflection.
Second, it is visible in coordinates: the vector \(s_i=(-1)^i\) cuts the
coordinate set into the even and odd fibers.  Paper 8's Observation 8.12 says
that these two languages meet only there.  All higher cyclotomic blocks have
dimension at least two and split, after complexification, into Fourier-plane
data rather than a coordinate bipartition.

This paper asks what remains true after replacing

$$
\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z
\qquad\text{by}\qquad
\mathbb F_q[t]/(f),
$$

or, more generally, by a finite quotient of a Dedekind domain.  The answer is
not obtained by copying the cyclic proof.  The additive group of
\(\mathbb F_q[t]/(f)\) is usually an elementary abelian \(p\)-group, not a
cyclic group with a distinguished generator.  The correct invariant is the
conductor of an additive character.  Cyclotomic blocks become conductor
blocks, ordinary Ramanujan sums become Hayes-type conductor sums, and the
integer half-turn kernel becomes a kernel problem for translations lying inside
the block-sign group.

In the language suggested by Rosas et al. [Rosas 2025], these conductor blocks
should also be read as information layers.  A quotient
\(\pi_{\mathfrak d}:R\to A/\mathfrak d\) defines a macrovariable, and the
cumulative conductor space below \(\mathfrak d\) is the tangent space of the
categorical subfamily visible to that quotient statistic.  Thus the
conductor-block theorem is not only a spectral decomposition of a simplex
frame; it is the arithmetic realization of a lattice of informationally
closed macrovariables, relative to quotient categorical subfamilies.  This
terminology is used cautiously: a conductor quotient is not sufficient for the
full microscopic categorical family unless it is injective.  It is sufficient
for the restricted model class that only sees the quotient.

The reward is that the \(\Phi_2\) uniqueness becomes a theorem rather than a
coincidence.  In any finite Dedekind quotient, the dimension of the conductor
block for \(\mathfrak d\) is

$$
\Phi_A(\mathfrak d)=|(A/\mathfrak d)^\times|.
$$

This equals \(1\) exactly when \(\mathfrak d\) is a squarefree product of
prime ideals of norm \(2\).  The primitive coordinate/reflection meeting points
are the individual norm-two primes.  In \(A=\mathbb F_q[t]\), this says:
one-dimensional nonconstant conductor blocks occur exactly for squarefree
products of distinct linear factors over \(\mathbb F_2\), and the primitive
ones are the individual linear factors.  Thus the function-field side exposes
the product structure hidden by the integer parent, where only the prime
\((2)\) can play this role.

## 2. Finite Conductor Quotients

Let \(A\) be a Dedekind domain and let \(I\subset A\) be a nonzero ideal such
that

$$
R=A/I
$$

is finite.  We write \(|R|=Q\).  The notation \(\mathfrak d\mid I\) means
that \(I\subseteq\mathfrak d\), so the quotient map \(A\to A/I\) further maps
to \(A/\mathfrak d\).  In the integer case this is the usual notation
\(d\mid N\); in the polynomial case it is the usual notation \(d\mid f\).

Assume \(R\) is equipped with a generating additive character

$$
\psi:R\to\mathbb C^\times
$$

such that

$$
\langle a,b\rangle_\psi=\psi(ab)
$$

identifies \(R\) with its additive character group.  Equivalently, every
additive character of \(R\) has the form

$$
\chi_b(a)=\psi(ab)
$$

for a unique \(b\in R\).  Finite quotients of Dedekind domains have this
Frobenius self-duality; the assumption records the chosen identification.

Let

$$
L^2(R)=\mathbb C^R,
\qquad
H_R=\left\{x\in\mathbb C^R:\sum_{a\in R}x_a=0\right\},
$$

with the standard Hermitian inner product.  For \(a\in R\), put

$$
v_a=e_a-Q^{-1}{\bf 1}.
$$

Then \(V_R=\{v_a:a\in R\}\) is the centered regular simplex in \(H_R\):

$$
\sum_{a\in R}v_a=0,
\qquad
\langle v_a,v_b\rangle=\delta_{ab}-Q^{-1}.
$$

After multiplying by \(\sqrt{Q/(Q-1)}\), it becomes a unit-norm regular
simplex frame for \(H_R\).

### 2.1 Conductors

An additive character \(\chi\) of \(R\) has conductor \(\mathfrak d\mid I\) if
it factors through \(A/\mathfrak d\) and is primitive there, meaning that it
does not factor through any proper divisor of \(\mathfrak d\).  The trivial
character has conductor \(1\).  For \(\mathfrak d\mid I\), define

$$
\widehat R[\mathfrak d]
=
\{\chi\in\widehat R:\operatorname{cond}(\chi)=\mathfrak d\}.
$$

The conductor block is

$$
H_R^{[\mathfrak d]}
=
\operatorname{span}\widehat R[\mathfrak d].
$$

Only \(\mathfrak d\ne 1\) appears in \(H_R\), because the \(\mathfrak d=1\)
block is the constant line \(\mathbb C{\bf 1}\).

### Theorem 2.1: Conductor-Block Decomposition

The zero-sum space decomposes orthogonally as

$$
H_R
=
\bigoplus_{\substack{\mathfrak d\mid I\\\mathfrak d\ne 1}}
H_R^{[\mathfrak d]}.
$$

Moreover,

$$
\dim H_R^{[\mathfrak d]}
=
\Phi_A(\mathfrak d)
:=
|(A/\mathfrak d)^\times|
$$

for every nontrivial conductor \(\mathfrak d\mid I\).

**Proof.**  The additive characters of the finite abelian group \(R\) form an
orthogonal basis of \(L^2(R)\).  The trivial character spans the constant line.
Every nontrivial character has a unique conductor, so partitioning the
nontrivial character basis by conductor gives the displayed orthogonal direct
sum.

A character of conductor \(\mathfrak d\) is a primitive additive character
modulo \(\mathfrak d\).  Under the chosen self-dual pairing, these primitive
characters are indexed by the units in \(A/\mathfrak d\).  Hence the number of
such characters is \(|(A/\mathfrak d)^\times|\), which is the displayed
dimension. \(\square\)

For \(h\in R\), define the conductor sum

$$
C_{\mathfrak d}(h)
=
\sum_{\chi\in\widehat R[\mathfrak d]}\chi(h).
$$

This is the generalized Ramanujan sum attached to the conductor
\(\mathfrak d\).

### Theorem 2.2: Projector Kernel Formula

Let \(P_{\mathfrak d}\) be the orthogonal projector onto
\(H_R^{[\mathfrak d]}\).  Then

$$
(P_{\mathfrak d})_{a,c}
=
Q^{-1}C_{\mathfrak d}(a-c)
$$

for all \(a,c\in R\).

**Proof.**  The normalized characters \(Q^{-1/2}\chi\) form an orthonormal
basis of \(L^2(R)\).  Projecting onto the span of the characters with conductor
\(\mathfrak d\) gives

$$
(P_{\mathfrak d})_{a,c}
=
Q^{-1}
\sum_{\operatorname{cond}(\chi)=\mathfrak d}
\chi(a)\overline{\chi(c)}.
$$

Since \(\chi\) is additive, \(\chi(a)\overline{\chi(c)}=\chi(a-c)\).  The sum
is exactly \(C_{\mathfrak d}(a-c)\). \(\square\)

The conductor sums also have a Mobius form.  Let \(\mu_A\) be the Mobius
function of the ideal divisor lattice.  For \(\mathfrak e\mid \mathfrak d\),
let \(h\equiv0\pmod{\mathfrak e}\) mean that the image of \(h\in A/I\) is zero
in \(A/\mathfrak e\).

### Theorem 2.3: Ideal-Mobius Formula

For every \(\mathfrak d\mid I\),

$$
C_{\mathfrak d}(h)
=
\sum_{\substack{\mathfrak e\mid\mathfrak d\\ h\equiv0\pmod{\mathfrak e}}}
|A/\mathfrak e|\,\mu_A(\mathfrak d/\mathfrak e).
$$

In a principal ideal domain this becomes

$$
C_d(h)
=
\sum_{e\mid\gcd(d,h)}
|A/(e)|\,\mu_A(d/e).
$$

**Proof.**  Let

$$
F_{\mathfrak e}(h)
=
\sum_{\chi\ \mathrm{factors\ through}\ A/\mathfrak e}\chi(h).
$$

By character orthogonality on \(A/\mathfrak e\),

$$
F_{\mathfrak e}(h)
=
\begin{cases}
|A/\mathfrak e|,&h\equiv0\pmod{\mathfrak e},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

Every character factoring through \(A/\mathfrak e\) has conductor
\(\mathfrak c\mid\mathfrak e\), so

$$
F_{\mathfrak e}(h)
=
\sum_{\mathfrak c\mid\mathfrak e}C_{\mathfrak c}(h).
$$

Mobius inversion on the divisor lattice gives

$$
C_{\mathfrak d}(h)
=
\sum_{\mathfrak e\mid\mathfrak d}
\mu_A(\mathfrak d/\mathfrak e)F_{\mathfrak e}(h),
$$

which is the first formula.  The principal ideal form rewrites the condition
\(h\equiv0\pmod e\) as \(e\mid h\). \(\square\)

## 3. Block-Sign Spectral Compounds

For each nontrivial \(\mathfrak d\mid I\), the projector \(P_{\mathfrak d}\)
is orthogonal to the other conductor projectors.  Hence any sign vector

$$
\epsilon=(\epsilon_{\mathfrak d})_{\mathfrak d\mid I,\ \mathfrak d\ne1},
\qquad
\epsilon_{\mathfrak d}\in\{\pm1\},
$$

defines an orthogonal self-adjoint operator on \(H_R\):

$$
T_\epsilon
=
\sum_{\substack{\mathfrak d\mid I\\\mathfrak d\ne1}}
\epsilon_{\mathfrak d}P_{\mathfrak d}.
$$

The block signs form an elementary abelian group

$$
B_R
\cong
(C_2)^{m_R},
$$

where \(m_R\) is the number of nontrivial conductors below \(I\).

Define the block-sign orbit of simplex sets by

$$
\mathcal O_R=\{T(V_R):T\in B_R\}.
$$

The kernel of this action is

$$
K_R
=
\{T\in B_R:T(V_R)=V_R\}.
$$

Let \(M_R=|B_R/K_R|\), and choose one representative from each coset.  The
spectral compound is

$$
X_R^{\mathrm{spec}}
=
\bigcup_{T\in B_R/K_R}T(V_R),
$$

with duplicate simplex sets removed.

### Theorem 3.1: Conductor-Sum Tight Frame

After unit normalization,

$$
\widehat X_R^{\mathrm{spec}}
=
\left\{\sqrt{\frac{Q}{Q-1}}\,x:
x\in X_R^{\mathrm{spec}}\right\}
$$

is a unit-norm tight frame for \(H_R\) with \(QM_R\) vectors and frame bound

$$
A_R=\frac{QM_R}{Q-1}.
$$

For unnormalized centered vertices,

$$
\langle T_\epsilon v_a,T_\eta v_b\rangle
=
Q^{-1}
\sum_{\substack{\mathfrak d\mid I\\\mathfrak d\ne1}}
\epsilon_{\mathfrak d}\eta_{\mathfrak d}
C_{\mathfrak d}(a-b).
$$

For unit-normalized vertices, the same expression is divided by \(Q-1\) rather
than \(Q\):

$$
\left\langle
\sqrt{\frac Q{Q-1}}T_\epsilon v_a,\,
\sqrt{\frac Q{Q-1}}T_\eta v_b
\right\rangle
=
\frac1{Q-1}
\sum_{\substack{\mathfrak d\mid I\\\mathfrak d\ne1}}
\epsilon_{\mathfrak d}\eta_{\mathfrak d}
C_{\mathfrak d}(a-b).
$$

**Proof.**  A unit-normalized regular simplex with \(Q\) vertices in the
\((Q-1)\)-dimensional space \(H_R\) has frame bound \(Q/(Q-1)\).  Each
\(T_\epsilon\) is orthogonal on \(H_R\), so every image \(T_\epsilon(V_R)\)
has the same normalized frame operator.  Summing over the \(M_R\) distinct
simplex images gives \(QM_R/(Q-1)\) times the identity.

For the cross-Gram formula, use commutativity and involutivity of the block
signs:

$$
\langle T_\epsilon v_a,T_\eta v_b\rangle
=
\langle v_a,T_{\epsilon\eta}v_b\rangle.
$$

Since \(T_{\epsilon\eta}=\sum_{\mathfrak d} \epsilon_{\mathfrak d}
\eta_{\mathfrak d}P_{\mathfrak d}\), and since \(P_{\mathfrak d}v_b
=P_{\mathfrak d}e_b\), Theorem 2.2 gives

$$
\langle v_a,P_{\mathfrak d}v_b\rangle
=
Q^{-1}C_{\mathfrak d}(a-b).
$$

Summing over conductors gives the unnormalized formula.  Multiplication by
\(Q/(Q-1)\) gives the unit-normalized formula. \(\square\)

The all-block sign is

$$
-I_{H_R}
=
\sum_{\mathfrak d\ne1}(-1)P_{\mathfrak d}.
$$

Thus the full block-sign compound is centrally symmetric unless \(-I\) lies
in the kernel and acts only as a relabeling of the original simplex.  As in
Paper 8, the ordinary coherence is often \(1\), and the useful packing
statistic is the non-antipodal projective angle set.

### Theorem 3.2: Kernel Criterion

Let \(\tau_b\) denote translation of coordinates by \(b\in R\):

$$
\tau_b(v_a)=v_{a+b}.
$$

Then

$$
K_R
=
B_R\cap\{\tau_b:b\in R\}.
$$

Equivalently, \(b\in R\) gives an element of \(K_R\) exactly when, for every
nontrivial conductor \(\mathfrak d\mid I\), the value \(\chi(b)\) is a constant
sign on all primitive characters \(\chi\) of conductor \(\mathfrak d\).

Distinct simplex sets in \(\mathcal O_R\) are vertex-disjoint.

**Proof.**  Every conductor projector is translation invariant, so every
\(T\in B_R\) commutes with every translation \(\tau_b\).  Suppose
\(T(V_R)=V_R\).  If \(T v_0=v_b\), then

$$
T v_a=T\tau_a v_0=\tau_aT v_0=v_{a+b}.
$$

Thus \(T\) acts on \(V_R\) as the translation \(\tau_b\), proving
\(K_R\subseteq B_R\cap\{\tau_b\}\).  The reverse inclusion is immediate.

In the character basis, \(\tau_b\) acts by multiplication by \(\chi(b)\) on
the \(\chi\)-line.  It lies in \(B_R\) exactly when that scalar is a constant
\(\pm1\) on each conductor block.  This proves the criterion.

Finally, if \(T(V_R)\) and \(S(V_R)\) share a vertex, then \(S^{-1}T\) sends
some \(v_a\) to some \(v_b\).  The same translation-commutation argument shows
that \(S^{-1}T(V_R)=V_R\), hence \(T(V_R)=S(V_R)\). \(\square\)

This theorem is the exact place where the function-field theory diverges from
Paper 8.  In the cyclic integer case, the kernel is trivial for odd \(N\) and
is generated by the half-turn for even \(N\).  For a general quotient, the
kernel is a separate translation-classification problem.

## 4. Integer Specialization

Take \(A=\mathbb Z\), \(I=(N)\), and \(R=\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z\).  The
conductor divisors are the positive divisors \(d\mid N\).  The conductor block
for \(d\) is the cyclotomic block

$$
H_N^{[d]}=\ker\Phi_d(C_N),
$$

and

$$
\dim H_N^{[d]}=\varphi(d).
$$

The conductor sum is the ordinary Ramanujan sum

$$
c_d(k)=\sum_{u\in(\mathbb Z/d\mathbb Z)^\times}e^{2\pi iuk/d}.
$$

The projector formula becomes

$$
(P_d)_{ij}=N^{-1}c_d(i-j),
$$

and the block-sign cross-Gram formula becomes

$$
\langle T_\epsilon v_i,T_\eta v_j\rangle
=
N^{-1}
\sum_{\substack{d\mid N\\d>1}}
\epsilon_d\eta_d c_d(i-j).
$$

After unit normalization, the denominator is \(N-1\).

### Theorem 4.1: Recovery of the Paper 8 Spectral Compound

For \(R=\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z\), Theorems 2.1, 2.2, 3.1, and 3.2 recover the
cyclotomic block-sign compound of Paper 8.  In particular:

1. conductor blocks are \(\Phi_d\)-cyclotomic blocks;
2. generalized conductor sums are ordinary Ramanujan sums;
3. the normalized cross-Gram entries are

$$
\frac1{N-1}
\sum_{\substack{d\mid N\\d>1}}
\epsilon_d\eta_d c_d(i-j);
$$

4. the kernel \(K_R\) is the subgroup of cyclic translations whose eigenvalues
are constant signs on every \(\Phi_d\)-block.

For \(N\) odd, \(K_R\) is trivial.  For \(N\) even, \(K_R\) is generated by
the half-turn \(i\mapsto i+N/2\), whose sign on \(H_N^{[d]}\) is
\((-1)^{N/d}\).

**Proof.**  The first three points are the specializations already displayed.
For the kernel, Theorem 3.2 says that a translation \(i\mapsto i+b\) lies in
\(K_R\) exactly when \(e^{2\pi ikb/N}\) is a constant sign on each primitive
frequency class \(k=(N/d)u\), \(u\in(\mathbb Z/d\mathbb Z)^\times\).  The only
cyclic translations with real symmetric permutation matrices are the identity
and, when \(N\) is even, the half-turn \(b=N/2\).  The half-turn acts on the
\(d\)-block by

$$
e^{2\pi i (N/d)u(N/2)/N}
=
e^{\pi iuN/d}
=
(-1)^{N/d},
$$

since \(u\) is coprime to \(d\).  This is exactly the Paper 8 kernel. \(\square\)

### Corollary 4.2: The Integer Meeting Point

Inside \(H_N\), the only possible nonconstant one-dimensional cyclotomic block
is \(\Phi_2\).  It occurs exactly when \(N\) is even.

**Proof.**  A cyclotomic block \(H_N^{[d]}\) has dimension \(\varphi(d)\).
The only positive integers with \(\varphi(d)=1\) are \(d=1\) and \(d=2\).  The
\(d=1\) block is the constant line and is removed by passing to \(H_N\).
Thus the only nonconstant one-dimensional block is \(d=2\), when \(2\mid N\).
\(\square\)

This is Paper 8's Observation 8.12 in its most compressed form.  It remains
the right closing observation for Paper 8 because that paper has just separated
coordinate-permutation rigidity from Fourier-plane reflection rigidity.  The
present paper turns the same sentence into a corollary of a broader conductor
theorem.

## 5. Function-Field Specialization

Let

$$
A=\mathbb F_q[t],
\qquad
f\in A\ \text{monic},
\qquad
R_f=A/(f),
\qquad
Q_f=|R_f|=q^{\deg f}.
$$

Fix a nontrivial additive character
\(\psi_p:\mathbb F_p\to\mathbb C^\times\).  If \(n=\deg f\), let
\(\lambda_f:R_f\to\mathbb F_q\) be the coefficient of \(t^{n-1}\) in the
unique representative of degree \(<n\).  Define

$$
\psi_f(x)
=
\psi_p\left(
\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb F_q/\mathbb F_p}(\lambda_f(x))
\right).
$$

The bilinear pairing

$$
(a,b)\mapsto\psi_f(ab)
$$

is nondegenerate.  Hence every additive character is

$$
\chi_b(a)=\psi_f(ab)
$$

for a unique \(b\in R_f\).

### Theorem 5.1: Function-Field Conductor Blocks

Let \(b\in A/(f)\), and write \(g=\gcd(f,b)\) using the monic representative
of the gcd.  Then

$$
\operatorname{cond}(\chi_b)=\frac f g.
$$

For every monic \(d\mid f\), define

$$
H_f^{[d]}
=
\operatorname{span}\{\chi_b:\operatorname{cond}(\chi_b)=d\}.
$$

Then

$$
H_{R_f}
=
\bigoplus_{\substack{d\mid f\\d\ne1}}H_f^{[d]},
\qquad
\dim H_f^{[d]}=\Phi_A(d)=|(A/(d))^\times|.
$$

The projector \(P_d\) onto \(H_f^{[d]}\) has kernel

$$
(P_d)_{a,c}=Q_f^{-1}C_d(a-c),
$$

where

$$
C_d(h)=\sum_{\operatorname{cond}(\chi)=d}\chi(h).
$$

**Proof.**  The coefficient functional \(\lambda_f\) makes \(A/(f)\) a
Frobenius algebra over \(\mathbb F_q\): if \(a\ne0\) has representative of
degree \(r<n\), then the coefficient of \(t^{n-1}\) in
\(a t^{n-1-r}\) is nonzero before any reduction modulo \(f\).  Thus
\((a,b)\mapsto\psi_f(ab)\) is nondegenerate after composing with the field
trace.

The character \(\chi_b\) factors through \(A/(d)\) exactly when it is trivial
on the ideal \((d)/(f)\), equivalently when \(bd\equiv0\pmod f\).  The minimal
monic \(d\) with this property is \(d=f/\gcd(f,b)\).  The decomposition,
dimension statement, and projector formula are then Theorems 2.1 and 2.2 in
the principal ideal domain \(A=\mathbb F_q[t]\). \(\square\)

### Theorem 5.2: Hayes-Mobius Formula

For \(d\mid f\),

$$
C_d(h)
=
\sum_{e\mid\gcd(d,h)}
|e|\,\mu(d/e),
$$

where \(|e|=q^{\deg e}\) and \(\mu\) is the polynomial Mobius function.  In
particular, if \(d\) is irreducible of degree \(m\), then

$$
C_d(h)
=
\begin{cases}
q^m-1,&d\mid h,\\
-1,&d\nmid h.
\end{cases}
$$

**Proof.**  This is Theorem 2.3 in the PID \(\mathbb F_q[t]\).  For
irreducible \(d\), the only divisors are \(1\) and \(d\), so the Mobius formula
gives \(-1\) unless \(d\mid h\), and \(-1+|d|=q^m-1\) when \(d\mid h\).
\(\square\)

Consequently, the function-field spectral compound has exact unit-normalized
inner products

$$
\frac1{Q_f-1}
\sum_{\substack{d\mid f\\d\ne1}}
\epsilon_d\eta_d C_d(a-b).
$$

This is the literal function-field replacement for the Ramanujan formula in
Paper 8.

### Carlitz-Hayes Framing

The quotient \(A/(f)\) is the \(f\)-torsion label set for the Carlitz module in
the same way that \(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z\) is the \(N\)-torsion label set for
\(\mathbb G_m\).  In this paper the Carlitz module is not used as extra
machinery; it is the correct altitude for the analogy.  Hayes sums are not
decorative substitutions for Ramanujan sums.  They are the conductor sums
attached to additive characters on the torsion quotient.

This is the precise scope of the present paper.  The proofs below use the
additive character theory and Hayes-Mobius conductor sums that already live on
the finite quotient \(A/(f)\).  They do not yet use the full Carlitz action to
enlarge the symmetry group, transport simplex images equivariantly through
torsion modules, or organize the Hayes character basis by a Carlitz-normalizer
action.  Problem 9.6 is therefore not a loose slogan: it is the next layer of
machinery after the conductor-block frame theorem, not an ingredient silently
used in it.

## 6. First Families and the Binary Meeting Point

### Theorem 6.1: Irreducible Quotients

If \(f\) is irreducible and \(Q_f>2\), then there is only one nontrivial
conductor block.  Hence

$$
B_{R_f}=\{\pm I_{H_{R_f}}\}
$$

and

$$
X_f^{\mathrm{spec}}
=
V_f\cup(-V_f).
$$

After unit normalization this is the antipodal regular simplex frame in
dimension \(Q_f-1\), with \(2Q_f\) vertices and frame bound

$$
\frac{2Q_f}{Q_f-1}.
$$

Its coordinate stabilizer contains the full symmetric group \(S_{Q_f}\), and
its full orthogonal stabilizer is \(C_2\times S_{Q_f}\) for \(Q_f>2\).

**Proof.**  If \(f\) is irreducible, the only divisors of \(f\) are \(1\) and
\(f\).  Thus \(H_{R_f}=H_f^{[f]}\), and the only block signs are \(I\) and
\(-I\).  The resulting frame is \(V_f\cup(-V_f)\).  The stabilizer statement is
the standard stabilizer of a regular simplex together with central inversion.
\(\square\)

### Theorem 6.2: Linear Conductors and Residue Fibers

Let \(\ell\mid f\) be linear over \(\mathbb F_q\).  The block
\(H_f^{[\ell]}\) has dimension \(q-1\), and its projector kernel is

$$
(P_\ell)_{a,c}
=
Q_f^{-1}
\begin{cases}
q-1,&a\equiv c\pmod\ell,\\
-1,&a\not\equiv c\pmod\ell.
\end{cases}
$$

Thus \(P_\ell\) sees exactly the \(q\) coordinate fibers of the residue map

$$
R_f\to A/(\ell)\cong\mathbb F_q.
$$

The block sign \(I-2P_\ell\) is a Householder reflection exactly when
\(q=2\).

**Proof.**  Since \(\ell\) is linear, \(|\ell|=q\) and
\(\dim H_f^{[\ell]}=|(A/(\ell))^\times|=q-1\).  The displayed kernel is the
irreducible-conductor formula in Theorem 5.2, applied to \(h=a-c\).  The block
is one-dimensional exactly when \(q-1=1\), i.e. \(q=2\). \(\square\)

Now we isolate the point that answers the Observation 8.12 placement question.

### Theorem 6.3: Binary Multi-Meeting-Point Theorem

Let \(R=A/I\) be a finite quotient of a Dedekind domain as in Section 2.  A
nonconstant conductor block \(H_R^{[\mathfrak d]}\) is one-dimensional if and
only if \(\mathfrak d\) is a squarefree product of prime ideals of norm \(2\).

The primitive coordinate/reflection meeting points are the individual norm-two
prime divisors \(\mathfrak p\mid I\).  Each such \(\mathfrak p\) gives a
one-dimensional conductor block whose two level sets are the two fibers of

$$
R\to A/\mathfrak p\cong\mathbb F_2.
$$

Products of distinct norm-two prime divisors give additional one-dimensional
conductor blocks.  These product lines are real reflection lines, but they are
not primitive coordinate quotients; they are products of the primitive binary
characters.

For \(A=\mathbb F_q[t]\), this says that one-dimensional nonconstant conductor
blocks occur exactly when \(q=2\) and \(d\) is a squarefree product of distinct
linear factors.  The primitive meeting points are the individual linear
factors.  In \(A=\mathbb F_2[t]\) itself there are at most two such finite
linear factors, \(t\) and \(t+1\); the theorem is stated in Dedekind form
because the mechanism is norm-two factorization, not the accidental size of a
particular polynomial ring.

**Proof.**  By Theorem 2.1,

$$
\dim H_R^{[\mathfrak d]}=|(A/\mathfrak d)^\times|.
$$

Factor

$$
\mathfrak d=\prod_i\mathfrak p_i^{e_i}.
$$

The Chinese remainder theorem gives

$$
|(A/\mathfrak d)^\times|
=
\prod_i
\left(
|A/\mathfrak p_i|^{e_i}
-
|A/\mathfrak p_i|^{e_i-1}
\right)
=
\prod_i
|A/\mathfrak p_i|^{e_i-1}
\left(|A/\mathfrak p_i|-1\right).
$$

Every factor is a positive integer.  The product equals \(1\) exactly when
each \(e_i=1\) and each residue field has size \(2\).  This proves the
squarefree norm-two criterion.

If \(\mathfrak p\) has norm \(2\), then \(A/\mathfrak p\cong\mathbb F_2\), so
the corresponding nontrivial additive character has values \(\pm1\) and cuts
the coordinate labels into the two fibers of \(R\to A/\mathfrak p\).  A product
of several such primes gives the product of the corresponding binary
characters.  It remains one-dimensional by the first paragraph, but it no
longer comes from a single residue map to \(\mathbb F_2\). \(\square\)

### Binary Meeting-Point Observation

The integer \(\Phi_2\) line is the one-prime case of Theorem 6.3.  Paper 8
sees only one nonconstant one-dimensional cyclotomic conductor because
\(\mathbb Z\) has only one norm-two prime, namely \((2)\).  A finite Dedekind
quotient can expose several primitive norm-two directions, and its split
binary products also expose the product conductor lines between them.  The
coordinate layer and the reflection layer therefore meet not at a universal
single line, but at the binary part of the conductor lattice.  The integer
\(\Phi_2\) uniqueness is the collapsed one-factor shadow of that statement.

### Proposition 6.4: The \(Q_f=4\) Atlas

Over \(\mathbb F_2\), quotients of size \(4\) already split into three
different conductor geometries:

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
f& R_f&\text{nontrivial block dimensions}&\text{role}\\
\hline
\text{irreducible quadratic}&\mathbb F_4&(3)&\text{irreducible antipodal simplex}\\
t^2&\mathbb F_2[u]/(u^2)&(1,2)&\text{one primitive binary line plus nilpotent block}\\
t(t+1)&\mathbb F_2\times\mathbb F_2&(1,1,1)&\text{two primitive lines plus one product line}.
\end{array}
$$

In all three cases the realized block-sign compound has two simplex images and
eight vertices.  After unit normalization its convex hull is the regular cube
in \(H_{R_f}\cong\mathbb R^3\), with \(f\)-vector

$$
(f_0,f_1,f_2)=(8,12,6),
$$

and its polar is the regular octahedron.  The common hull hides different
conductor mechanisms.  Externally, the coordinate stabilizer is \(S_4\) in all
three cases and the full orthogonal stabilizer is the cube group
\(C_2\times S_4\), of order \(48\).  Projectively, the eight vertices give the
unique tetrahedral four-line equiangular set in \(\mathbb R^3\), with common
absolute inner product \(1/3\).

1. for irreducible \(f\), \(B_{R_f}\cong C_2\) and \(K_{R_f}=1\);
2. for \(f=t^2\), \(B_{R_f}\cong C_2^2\) and \(K_{R_f}\cong C_2\), generated
   by the nilpotent translation;
3. for \(f=t(t+1)\), \(B_{R_f}\cong C_2^3\) and
   \(K_{R_f}\cong C_2^2\), the full translation group acting as the even
   sign subgroup on the three binary character lines.

Thus the split case is the first place where Theorem 6.3 multiplies inside a
finite atlas: the two primitive binary lines and their product line all occur,
but the block-sign orbit collapses through a rank-two translation kernel.

All Gram matrices below are unit-normalized, with rows and columns indexed by
the displayed coordinate order.  The within-simplex matrix is always

$$
G_+
=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&-1/3&-1/3&-1/3\\
-1/3&1&-1/3&-1/3\\
-1/3&-1/3&1&-1/3\\
-1/3&-1/3&-1/3&1
\end{pmatrix},
$$

and the antipodal cross-Gram matrix is

$$
G_-
=
\begin{pmatrix}
-1&1/3&1/3&1/3\\
1/3&-1&1/3&1/3\\
1/3&1/3&-1&1/3\\
1/3&1/3&1/3&-1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For an irreducible quadratic \(f\), write
\(R_f=\mathbb F_2[\alpha]\) with \(\alpha^2+\alpha+1=0\), and order the
coordinates as \(0,1,\alpha,1+\alpha\).  The three nontrivial additive
characters all have conductor \(f\).  Thus \(B_{R_f}=\{I,-I\}\).  Nonzero
translations have mixed signs on the three primitive character lines:

$$
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
b&\chi_1(b)&\chi_\alpha(b)&\chi_{1+\alpha}(b)\\
\hline
0&+&+&+\\
1&+&-&-\\
\alpha&-&-&+\\
1+\alpha&-&+&-
\end{array}
$$

so Theorem 3.2 gives \(K_{R_f}=1\).  The only nontrivial cross-Gram matrix is
\(G_-\).

For the nilpotent quotient \(R_f=\mathbb F_2[u]/(u^2)\), order the coordinates
as \(0,1,u,1+u\), and take the generating character
\(\lambda(a+bu)=(-1)^b\).  The conductor-\(u\) line is spanned by
\(\chi_u(a+bu)=(-1)^a\), while the conductor-\(u^2\) plane is spanned by
\(\chi_1\) and \(\chi_{1+u}\).  Translation signs are

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|cc}
b&\chi_u(b)&\chi_1(b)&\chi_{1+u}(b)\\
\hline
0&+&+&+\\
1&-&+&-\\
u&+&-&-\\
1+u&-&-&+
\end{array}
$$

The rows constant on each conductor block are \(0\) and \(u\), hence
\(K_{R_f}=\{I,\tau_u\}\).  In block signs,
\(\tau_u\) is \(+1\) on \(H^{[u]}\) and \(-1\) on \(H^{[u^2]}\).  Therefore
the conductor-\(u\) reflection \(\rho_u=I-2P_u\) satisfies

$$
\rho_u v_b=-v_{b+u}.
$$

Its labeled cross-Gram matrix is

$$
G_u
=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/3&1/3&-1&1/3\\
1/3&1/3&1/3&-1\\
-1&1/3&1/3&1/3\\
1/3&-1&1/3&1/3
\end{pmatrix},
$$

which is \(G_-\) after the column relabeling \(b\mapsto b+u\).  The remaining
nontrivial block sign is the kernel element \(\tau_u\), so the distinct
simplex images are represented by \(V_f\) and \(\rho_u(V_f)=-V_f\).

For the split quotient \(R_f\cong\mathbb F_2\times\mathbb F_2\), write
coordinates as \(00,10,01,11\), where the two entries are the residues modulo
\(t\) and \(t+1\).  The three character lines are

$$
\chi_t(x,y)=(-1)^x,\qquad
\chi_{t+1}(x,y)=(-1)^y,\qquad
\chi_f(x,y)=(-1)^{x+y}.
$$

The translation sign table is

$$
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
b&\chi_t(b)&\chi_{t+1}(b)&\chi_f(b)\\
\hline
00&+&+&+\\
10&-&+&-\\
01&+&-&-\\
11&-&-&+
\end{array}
$$

Since every conductor block is a line, every row is constant on every block.
Thus Theorem 3.2 gives

$$
K_{R_f}
=
\{\tau_{00},\tau_{10},\tau_{01},\tau_{11}\}
\cong C_2^2,
$$

the even sign subgroup of \(B_{R_f}\cong C_2^3\).  The quotient \(B_{R_f}/K_R\)
is detected by the parity of the number of binary lines flipped.

The three single-line reflections already show the multi-meeting-point
geometry.  Their labeled cross-Gram matrices are

$$
G_t
=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/3&1/3&-1&1/3\\
1/3&1/3&1/3&-1\\
-1&1/3&1/3&1/3\\
1/3&-1&1/3&1/3
\end{pmatrix},
$$

$$
G_{t+1}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/3&-1&1/3&1/3\\
-1&1/3&1/3&1/3\\
1/3&1/3&1/3&-1\\
1/3&1/3&-1&1/3
\end{pmatrix},
$$

and

$$
G_f
=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/3&1/3&1/3&-1\\
1/3&1/3&-1&1/3\\
1/3&-1&1/3&1/3\\
-1&1/3&1/3&1/3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\rho_t v_b=-v_{b+01},\qquad
\rho_{t+1}v_b=-v_{b+10},\qquad
\rho_fv_b=-v_{b+11}.
$$

The two primitive lines \(t,t+1\) and the product line \(f=t(t+1)\) therefore
give three distinct labeled reflections, but all odd block signs represent
the same nontrivial coset in \(B_{R_f}/K_R\).  The realized compound is again
\(V_f\cup(-V_f)\).

**Proof.**  The block dimensions are
\(|(A/(d))^\times|\), so the three rows of the dimension table follow from
Theorem 2.1.  The displayed translation sign tables apply Theorem 3.2 directly:
a translation lies in \(K_R\) exactly when its signs are constant on each
conductor block.  This gives the three kernels listed above.

The Gram matrices follow from Theorem 3.1.  For a one-dimensional binary
character line \(\chi\), the line-flip cross-Gram entry is

$$
\langle \widehat v_a,\rho_\chi\widehat v_b\rangle
=
\frac43
\left(
\delta_{a,b}-\frac14-\frac12\chi(a)\chi(b)
\right).
$$

It equals \(-1\) precisely when \(a\ne b\) and \(a,b\) lie in the same binary
fiber, and it equals \(1/3\) otherwise.  This gives \(G_u\), \(G_t\),
\(G_{t+1}\), and \(G_f\).  The all-block sign gives \(G_-\).

Finally, in every case the nontrivial coset of \(B_R/K_R\) is represented by
central inversion up to a translation relabeling.  Thus the realized vertex
set is \(V_f\cup(-V_f)\).  In the orthonormal character coordinates, the
normalized vertices are four alternating vertices of the cube
\(\{(\pm1,\pm1,\pm1)/\sqrt3\}\), and adjoining their negatives gives the full
cube. \(\square\)

### Corollary 6.5: Rank-Two Binary Reflection-Kernel Duality

Let \(R=A/(\mathfrak p\mathfrak q)\), where \(\mathfrak p\) and
\(\mathfrak q\) are distinct norm-two prime ideals in a Dedekind domain.
Then

$$
R\cong\mathbb F_2\times\mathbb F_2,
$$

and the three nonconstant conductor blocks
\(H_R^{[\mathfrak p]}\), \(H_R^{[\mathfrak q]}\), and
\(H_R^{[\mathfrak p\mathfrak q]}\) are lines.  For each nonconstant conductor
\(\mathfrak d\), there is a unique nonzero kernel translation
\(k_{\mathfrak d}\in R\), with \(\tau_{k_{\mathfrak d}}\in K_R\), whose
translation sign is \(+1\) on
\(H_R^{[\mathfrak d]}\) and \(-1\) on the other two conductor lines.
Equivalently, the block sign \(-\tau_{k_{\mathfrak d}}\) has the unique
\(-1\)-sign on \(H_R^{[\mathfrak d]}\), and

$$
\rho_{\mathfrak d}v_b
=
(I-2P_{\mathfrak d})v_b
=
-v_{b+k_{\mathfrak d}}.
$$

The map

$$
\mathfrak d\longmapsto k_{\mathfrak d}
$$

is a bijection from the three nonconstant squarefree binary conductors to
the three nonidentity kernel translations, and it is additive in the rank-two
binary lattice:

$$
k_{\mathfrak p}+k_{\mathfrak q}
=
k_{\mathfrak p\mathfrak q}.
$$

In the split polynomial coordinates of Proposition 6.4 this is exactly

$$
k_t=01,\qquad
k_{t+1}=10,\qquad
k_{t(t+1)}=11.
$$

**Proof.**  The CRT identifies \(R\) with the two-dimensional vector space
\(\mathbb F_2^2\).  Its three nontrivial conductor characters are the three
nonzero linear functionals on \(R\).  If \(\chi_{\mathfrak d}\) is one of
them, its kernel is a one-dimensional subspace of \(R\), hence contains a
unique nonzero element \(k_{\mathfrak d}\).  Therefore
\(\chi_{\mathfrak d}(k_{\mathfrak d})=+1\), while the other two nontrivial
characters take value \(-1\) on \(k_{\mathfrak d}\).  This proves the sign
claim and gives \(\rho_{\mathfrak d}=-\tau_{k_{\mathfrak d}}\) on \(H_R\).

For additivity, let the two primitive characters be \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\).
Then \(k_\alpha\) is killed by \(\alpha\) and not by \(\beta\), while
\(k_\beta\) is killed by \(\beta\) and not by \(\alpha\).  Hence
\(k_\alpha+k_\beta\) is killed by \(\alpha+\beta\) and is nonzero, so it is
the kernel element attached to the product character.  Thus the three
rank-two binary reflections are distinct as labeled conductor reflections but
represent the same nontrivial coset of \(B_R/K_R\). \(\square\)

This duality is a rank-two boundary phenomenon.  For an all-binary quotient
\(R\cong\mathbb F_2^k\), the translation kernel inside
\(B_R\cong\mathbb F_2^{2^k-1}\) is the binary simplex code
\([2^k-1,k,2^{k-1}]\); its cosets are the usual Hamming-side quotient.  When
\(k=2\), this quotient has codimension \(1\), so a single conductor line
determines a unique nonzero kernel translation.  For \(k\ge3\), the
rank-two bijection is replaced by the full simplex-code/Hamming-code
decomposition.

### Number-Ring Realization

The split \(Q_f=4\) row is not special to the polynomial model.  Let

$$
K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-7}),\qquad
\omega=\frac{1+\sqrt{-7}}2,\qquad
\mathcal O_K=\mathbb Z[\omega].
$$

Then \(\omega\) satisfies

$$
X^2-X+2=0,
$$

and modulo \(2\),

$$
X^2-X+2\equiv X^2+X=X(X+1).
$$

Thus

$$
\mathcal O_K/(2)
\cong
\mathbb F_2[X]/(X^2+X)
\cong
\mathbb F_2\times\mathbb F_2.
$$

Equivalently, \((2)\) splits as the product of the two norm-two prime ideals

$$
(2,\omega)\qquad\text{and}\qquad(2,\omega+1).
$$

Therefore the split atlas and Corollary 6.5 apply word-for-word to
\(\mathcal O_K/(2)\).  This is the smallest quadratic number-ring realization,
by absolute discriminant, of the split binary conductor lattice.  More
generally, every quadratic field with fundamental discriminant
\(D\equiv1\pmod 8\) gives a two-prime binary quotient at \((2)\).

This matters for the interpretation of Theorem 6.3.  The integer ring
\(\mathbb Z\) sees only one norm-two prime, so Paper 8 has only the
\(\Phi_2\) line.  A larger number ring in which \((2)\) splits sees several
\(\Phi_2\)-analog lines and their product lines.  The binary
multi-meeting-point theorem is therefore already a number-theoretic
phenomenon, not a function-field substitute.

For all-binary quotients \(R\cong\mathbb F_2^k\), the realized polytope itself
is classical: in the orthogonal character basis, the full block-sign orbit is
the \((2^k-1)\)-cube.  This is the Slepian group-orbit construction for the
binary simplex code, and its coset decomposition is the first-order
Reed-Muller/simplex-code decomposition studied by Sloane and Dick
[Slepian; MacWilliams-Sloane; Sloane-Dick].  Conway and Sloane give the
general construction of spherical codes from binary codes, including the full
binary code as the cube vertex set [Conway-Sloane, Chap. 1, Sec. 2.5].  The
new information supplied by Theorem 6.3 is the arithmetic origin of that code:
the cube axes are indexed by squarefree norm-two conductors, the translation
kernel is \(K_R\), and the primitive/product distinction comes from the
conductor lattice rather than from an undifferentiated coordinate set.

The first all-binary case beyond the rank-two collapse has three norm-two
prime factors:

$$
R\cong\mathbb F_2^3.
$$

This cannot occur in \(A=\mathbb F_q[t]\): for \(q=2\) there are only two
linear factors, and for \(q>2\) the linear factors have norm \(q>2\).  It does
occur in number rings where \((2)\) splits completely.  A concrete cyclic
cubic realization, in the conductor-\(31\) family
[Silvester-Spearman-Williams], is useful enough to record.  Let \(\theta\) be
a root of

$$
x^3+x^2-10x-8,
$$

and set \(K=\mathbb Q(\theta)\).  The polynomial is irreducible, has
discriminant \(4\cdot31^2\), and

$$
\beta=\frac{\theta^2+\theta}{2}
$$

is integral; indeed it satisfies \(x^3-10x^2+23x+2=0\).  The basis
\(\{1,\theta,\beta\}\) has discriminant \(31^2\), so it is an integral basis
for \(\mathcal O_K\).  Modulo \(2\), the multiplication relations are

$$
\theta^2=\theta,\qquad
\beta^2=\beta,\qquad
\theta\beta=\theta.
$$

Thus the three elements

$$
e_1=\theta,\qquad e_2=\beta-\theta,\qquad e_3=1-\beta
$$

are orthogonal idempotents summing to \(1\), and

$$
\mathcal O_K/(2)\cong
\mathbb F_2e_1\oplus\mathbb F_2e_2\oplus\mathbb F_2e_3
\cong\mathbb F_2^3.
$$

This gives the promised degree-three number-ring instance of the rank-three
binary conductor lattice.

### Proposition 6.6: The Rank-Three Binary Atlas

Let \(R=\mathbb F_2^3\).  Then all seven nonconstant conductor blocks are
one-dimensional, indexed by the seven nonzero characters

$$
\chi_m(x)=(-1)^{m\cdot x},
\qquad
m\in\mathbb F_2^3\setminus\{0\}.
$$

Consequently

$$
B_R\cong C_2^7,\qquad
K_R\cong R\cong C_2^3,\qquad
M_R=|B_R/K_R|=2^{7-3}=16.
$$

The spectral compound has \(16\cdot8=128\) labeled vertices in dimension \(7\).
In the orthogonal character basis it is the \(7\)-cube, decomposed into \(16\)
arithmetic simplex images.

More explicitly, order

$$
\mathbb F_2^3=
\{000,100,010,001,110,101,011,111\}.
$$

Represent the quotient \(B_R/K_R\) by the functions

$$
q_\alpha(m)
=
a_{12}m_1m_2+a_{13}m_1m_3+a_{23}m_2m_3+a_{123}m_1m_2m_3,
$$

where

$$
\alpha=(a_{12},a_{13},a_{23},a_{123})\in\mathbb F_2^4.
$$

Let \(\epsilon_\alpha(m)=(-1)^{q_\alpha(m)}\).  The cross-Gram matrix from the
base simplex to the image represented by \(\alpha\) is translation-circulant:

$$
G_\alpha(a,b)
=
\frac1{7}\kappa_\alpha(a-b),
\qquad
\kappa_\alpha(h)
=
\sum_{m\ne0}\epsilon_\alpha(m)(-1)^{m\cdot h}.
$$

The following table gives \(\kappa_\alpha(h)\) in the displayed order of
\(h\)'s; dividing by \(7\) gives the actual matrix entries.

$$
\begin{array}{c|rrrrrrrr}
\alpha&000&100&010&001&110&101&011&111\\
\hline
0000& 7&-1&-1&-1&-1&-1&-1&-1\\
0001& 5& 1& 1& 1&-3&-3&-3& 1\\
0010& 3&-1& 3& 3&-1&-1&-5&-1\\
0011& 5&-3& 1& 1& 1& 1&-3&-3\\
0100& 3& 3&-1& 3&-1&-5&-1&-1\\
0101& 5& 1&-3& 1& 1&-3& 1&-3\\
0110& 3&-1&-1& 3& 3&-1&-1&-5\\
0111& 1& 1& 1& 5& 1&-3&-3&-3\\
1000& 3& 3& 3&-1&-5&-1&-1&-1\\
1001& 5& 1& 1&-3&-3& 1& 1&-3\\
1010& 3&-1& 3&-1&-1& 3&-1&-5\\
1011& 1& 1& 5& 1&-3& 1&-3&-3\\
1100& 3& 3&-1&-1&-1&-1& 3&-5\\
1101& 1& 5& 1& 1&-3&-3& 1&-3\\
1110&-1& 3& 3& 3&-1&-1&-1&-5\\
1111& 1& 1& 1& 1& 1& 1& 1&-7
\end{array}
$$

The first row is the ordinary within-simplex Gram kernel.  The last row is
the antipodal simplex after translation by \(111\).  The other fourteen rows
are the genuinely new arithmetic simplex images between these two extremes.

**Proof.**  Since every nonzero character of \(R\) has conductor a squarefree
product of norm-two prime factors, every nonconstant conductor block is a
line.  This gives \(B_R\cong C_2^7\).  A translation by \(b\in R\) acts on the
character line \(\chi_m\) by the sign \(\chi_m(b)\).  The seven signs
\(m\mapsto\chi_m(b)\) determine \(b\), so the translation kernel has order
\(8\) and is \(C_2^3\).  Hence \(M_R=2^7/2^3=16\).

Choosing the character basis identifies the centered simplex vertices with
the eight sign columns

$$
v_a=\frac1{\sqrt7}\bigl(\chi_m(a)\bigr)_{m\ne0}.
$$

The full block-sign orbit is therefore the full sign cube in these seven
coordinates.  Quotienting by translations is exactly quotienting sign
functions on the seven nonzero \(m\)'s by the three-dimensional space of
linear functions \(m\mapsto m\cdot b\).  The four monomials
\(m_1m_2,m_1m_3,m_2m_3,m_1m_2m_3\) give a complementary set of representatives,
and the displayed Walsh sums are precisely
\(\sum_{m\ne0}\epsilon_\alpha(m)(-1)^{m\cdot(a-b)}\). \(\square\)

## 7. Divisor Schemes and Coordinate Colors

The cross-Gram formula implies a natural coordinate-pair coloring.  For a
difference \(h=a-b\in R\), define its conductor-color vector by

$$
\mathcal C(h)
=
\left(C_{\mathfrak d}(h)\right)_{\mathfrak d\mid I,\ \mathfrak d\ne1}.
$$

The exact inner products between block-sign vertices are obtained from this
vector by changing signs and summing.

### Theorem 7.1: Divisor-Scheme Fusion

Let \(R=A/I\) be a finite Dedekind quotient as above.  For every unit
\(u\in R^\times\),

$$
C_{\mathfrak d}(uh)=C_{\mathfrak d}(h)
$$

for all \(\mathfrak d\mid I\) and all \(h\in R\).  Therefore the coordinate
colors \(\mathcal C(h)\) are constant on unit orbits of differences.  In
particular, the coordinate-pair color relation of the spectral compound is a
fusion of the Schurian association scheme of the affine unit action

$$
R\rtimes R^\times
\curvearrowright R.
$$

In the polynomial case, the colors are functions of the ideal gcds
\(\gcd(d,h)\) appearing in the Hayes-Mobius formula.

**Proof.**  Multiplication by a unit permutes primitive characters of any fixed
conductor:

$$
\chi(h)\mapsto\chi(uh)
$$

is the same as replacing \(\chi\) by the primitive character
\(x\mapsto\chi(ux)\).  Hence the conductor sum is unchanged:

$$
C_{\mathfrak d}(uh)
=
\sum_{\operatorname{cond}(\chi)=\mathfrak d}\chi(uh)
=
\sum_{\operatorname{cond}(\chi)=\mathfrak d}\chi(h).
$$

Thus the conductor-color vector is constant on \(R^\times\)-orbits of
differences.  The affine unit action has orbitals indexed by such unit orbits
of differences, so the conductor coloring is a fusion of that Schurian scheme.
For \(A=\mathbb F_q[t]\), Theorem 5.2 gives the explicit gcd dependence.
\(\square\)

This is the safe general replacement for the divisor-fusion theorem of Paper
8.  Determining when these colors already determine the full coordinate
stabilizer of \(X_R^{\mathrm{spec}}\) is a separate classification problem.

## 8. What Does Not Transfer for Free

The formal conductor theory above is robust.  Several geometric conclusions
from Paper 8 are not formal consequences of it.

1. **The cyclic generator.**  In \(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z\), all translations are
generated by one shift \(C_N\).  In \(\mathbb F_q[t]/(f)\), the additive group
is usually a vector space over \(\mathbb F_p\).  Arguments that use a single
cyclic generator must be rewritten in terms of the full translation group
(Problem 9.2).

2. **The half-turn kernel.**  Paper 8 has a unique nontrivial kernel element
for even \(N\), the cyclic half-turn.  In general, the kernel is
\(B_R\cap R\) under the translation representation.  Theorem 3.2 gives the
criterion, not a closed classification (Problem 9.3).

3. **The \(N=4\) stella boundary.**  Proposition 6.4 computes the quotient-size
\(Q_f=4\) trichotomy over \(\mathbb F_2\), and Corollary 6.5 identifies the
rank-two split-binary reflection-kernel duality behind the split row.  The
rank-three all-binary atlas in Proposition 6.6 shows what happens after that
rank-two collapse disappears.  The lesson is that the geometry must be indexed
by conductor factorization, not by \(Q_f\) alone.  The next atlas problem is
to add mixed binary/non-binary conductor types (Problem 9.4).

4. **Convex hull product formulas.**  Paper 8's parity prism/free-sum
description uses a one-dimensional parity line and two equal coordinate
fibers.  A linear conductor over \(\mathbb F_q\) has dimension \(q-1\), and a
nonreduced quotient has nilpotent conductor layers.  Convex-hull structure is
a new problem (Problem 9.5).

5. **Fourier-plane reflection normalizers.**  The \(G(d,d,2)\)-type refinements
in Paper 8 use cyclic translation eigenvalues and conjugate Fourier planes
inside \(\Phi_d\)-blocks.  In the function-field setting the conductor
organization is Hayes/Carlitz, while additive character values have residue
characteristic \(p\).  The replacement object should be the monomial
normalizer of the Hayes character system, not a copied Shephard-Todd factor
(Problem 9.1).

## 9. Problems Beyond Formal Transfer

Let

$$
\mathcal X_f
=
\{\chi_b:b\in A/(f),\ b\ne0\}
$$

be the nontrivial additive character basis of \(H_{R_f}\).  The conductor
partition splits \(\mathcal X_f\) into subsets

$$
\mathcal X_f[d]
=
\{\chi_b:\operatorname{cond}(\chi_b)=d\}.
$$

The block-sign group changes signs on the spans of these subsets.  The
translation group acts diagonally on individual character lines.  The unit
group acts by permuting character labels.  These generate a natural monomial
group on the Hayes character basis.

**Problem 9.1: Hayes Monomial Normalizer.**  Determine the monomial normalizer
of the Hayes character system: the unitary monomial transformations of
\(\mathbb C\mathcal X_f\) that preserve the conductor partition, normalize the
translation action, and preserve the block-sign spectral compound.  Identify
its reflection-generated subgroup, and determine when additional Galois,
Frobenius, or CRT factor permutations are not generated by reflections.

This is the function-field replacement for Paper 8's Fourier-plane refinement
problem.  It is deliberately stated as a problem because the cyclic proof does
not transfer: the arithmetic normalizer is now controlled by conductor
characters and residue-field structure, not by a single root-of-unity order.

**Problem 9.2: Translation-Module Replacement for the Cyclic Generator.**
Rebuild the cyclic-generator arguments of Paper 8 using the full translation
module of \(R=A/I\).  The desired replacement should identify which parts of
the block-circulant Gram calculus, coordinate-pair orbitals, and stabilizer
proofs depend only on the regular translation action, and which parts depend
essentially on having a single generator \(C_N\).  In the polynomial case this
means working naturally with the additive \(\mathbb F_p\)-module
\(\mathbb F_q[t]/(f)\), not with an artificially chosen ordering of its
coordinates.

**Problem 9.3: Translation Kernel Classification.**  Classify the finite
kernel

$$
K_R=B_R\cap\{\tau_b:b\in R\}
$$

from Theorem 3.2 in conductor-theoretic terms.  Equivalently, determine all
\(b\in R\) for which every primitive character of a fixed conductor takes the
same sign on \(b\).  The answer should recover the integer trivial/half-turn
dichotomy, the nilpotent \(Q_f=4\) kernel \(\{0,u\}\), and the split
\(Q_f=4\) even-sign kernel \(C_2^2\), while also explaining higher
nilpotent, CRT, and mixed-conductor quotients.

**Problem 9.4: Binary and Small-Quotient Atlases.**  Extend Propositions 6.4
and 6.6 from the \(Q=4\) and split \(Q=8\) base cases to the first genuinely
new mixed quotients.  For any quotient with several norm-two prime factors
and at least one non-binary conductor component, compute the Boolean conductor
lattice, the primitive binary reflection lines, their product lines, the
translation kernel \(K_R\), the resulting cross-Gram schemes, coordinate
stabilizers, and convex hulls.  In this form, the integer \(\Phi_2\) line
should appear explicitly as the one-factor shadow of the binary
multi-meeting-point theorem, while the higher blocks should show how the
binary cube decomposition interacts with non-binary Hayes conductor blocks.
The next clean targets are \(R\cong\mathbb F_2^3\times\mathbb F_4\),
nilpotent binary quotients of size \(8\), and number-ring quotients in which
\((2)\) has both split and ramified norm-two factors.

**Problem 9.5: Mixed-Conductor Convex Hulls.**  Determine the face lattices and
structural decompositions of \(P_R^{\mathrm{spec}}=\operatorname{conv}
X_R^{\mathrm{spec}}\) for Dedekind quotients with more than one conductor
type: split squarefree factors, nilpotent factors, and products of the two.
The target is a replacement for Paper 8's prism/free-sum and polar
intersection results, but indexed by conductor lattices rather than by a
cyclic divisor chain.  The \(Q_f=4\) cube is the boundary case, and
Proposition 6.6 records the all-binary \(7\)-cube; the first mixed tests
should decide which conductor blocks become prism directions, which become
free-sum factors, and which remain coupled through CRT or nilpotent layers.

**Problem 9.6: Carlitz-Equivariant Spectral Compounds.**  Promote the
Carlitz-Hayes framing from altitude to machinery.  Construct the conductor
block spectral compound as a Carlitz-equivariant object, identify the enlarged
symmetry group acting on Hayes characters and conductor blocks, and determine
how that equivariance changes \(K_R\), block-sign normalizers, reflection
subgroups, and convex hull structure.  This is the point where the
function-field theory should stop merely paralleling the integer parent and
become its own equivariant geometry.

**Problem 9.7: Prime Cyclic Fisher-Cubic Benchmark.**  Specialize the
conductor-block theorem to \(R=\mathbb Z/\ell\mathbb Z\) for prime \(\ell\),
with \(\ell=7\) as the weekday benchmark.  Since there is only one
nontrivial conductor packet, the conductor lattice has no internal block
splitting: all off-centroid Fisher coupling is carried by one
\((\ell-1)\)-dimensional additive-character packet.  Compute the raised
Amari-Chentsov operator

$$
K^{CH}_{p_*}(h)v=\ell\,h*v-\ell\langle h,v\rangle p_*
$$

in the additive-character basis and record its convolution matrix.  This is
the clean control case for the later mixed-conductor theory: if the prime
cyclic simplex already explains an empirical linear-versus-manifold gap, the
effect is intrinsic behavior curvature; if it does not, the residual must
come from activation-side pullback distortion, spline parameterization, or
non-C\(_\ell\) output directions such as an `other' bin.

**Problem 9.8: Conductor Macrovariable Closure Taxonomy.**  Reinterpret the
finite conductor atlases as lattices of Rosas-style macrovariables.  For each
finite quotient \(R=A/I\) and each conductor quotient
\(\pi_{\mathfrak d}:R\to A/\mathfrak d\), distinguish:

1. partition closure: the fibers of \(\pi_{\mathfrak d}\) form an orbit or
   quotient partition;
2. sufficient-statistic closure: the quotient is sufficient for the
   \({\mathcal F}_{\mathfrak d}\)-measurable categorical subfamily;
3. normalizer closure: the Hayes/Carlitz normalizer preserves the layer;
4. adjacency-algebra closure: the quotient-character adjacency operators
   internal to that layer close without using hidden filtered data.

The first two notions belong already to the conductor-block spectral theorem.
The third belongs to the Hayes normalizer problems.  The fourth is deliberately
deferred to Papers 10 and 11, where off-centroid Fisher couplings can reveal
that a conductor layer is closed at the graded level but not at the full
filtered adjacency-algebra level.

## 10. Conclusion

The general conductor-block theorem gives a common spine for the integer,
function-field, and number-ring stories.  The regular simplex over a finite
quotient ring
decomposes by additive-character conductor; the conductor projectors have
generalized Ramanujan kernels; and block-sign images of the simplex form an
exact unit-norm tight frame after duplicate simplex images are removed.  The
integer case recovers Paper 8's cyclotomic block-sign compound and its
Ramanujan cross-Gram formula.  The function-field case replaces \(\Phi_d\) by
Carlitz-Hayes conductor blocks and replaces \(c_d\) by the polynomial
Ramanujan sum \(C_d\).

The placement of Observation 8.12 is now clean.  In Paper 8 it remains the
right closing observation: within the cyclic integer simplex, the coordinate
layer and Fourier-plane reflection layer meet only at \(\Phi_2\).  In this
paper it becomes the first corollary of a broader binary theorem:
one-dimensional nonconstant conductor blocks occur exactly at squarefree
norm-two conductors, and the primitive meeting points are the individual
norm-two factors.  Split binary quotients can therefore display several
primitive meeting points, plus product conductor lines between them.  The
integer \(\Phi_2\) uniqueness is the one-factor collapse of that richer
conductor lattice.

The \(Q_f=4\) atlas is the first place where this statement is computed rather
than only named.  The irreducible, nilpotent, and split quotients all realize
the same final cube \(V_f\cup(-V_f)\), but their conductor explanations differ:
one three-dimensional irreducible block, one binary line plus a nilpotent
plane, and three binary lines whose even-sign subgroup is the full translation
kernel.  Thus the split case already shows the binary multi-meeting-point
mechanism under a visible collapse of simplex images.  The same split row is
realized by \(\mathcal O_{\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-7})}/(2)\), so this is not merely a
function-field atlas: it is the first number-ring example of multiple
\(\Phi_2\)-analog conductor lines.

The rank-three binary atlas makes the same point without the rank-two
collapse.  In \(R\cong\mathbb F_2^3\), the seven nonconstant conductor blocks
are seven binary lines, \(K_R\cong C_2^3\), and the quotient \(B_R/K_R\) has
sixteen simplex images.  The resulting \(128\)-vertex compound is the
classical \(7\)-cube in character coordinates, but now with its arithmetic
decomposition exposed: primitive norm-two lines, product conductor lines, and
simplex-image cosets of the translation kernel.  The explicit cyclic cubic
field of conductor \(31\) above realizes this case over a number ring.

The remaining work is no longer a formal transfer.  The translation kernel
\(K_R\), cyclic-generator replacements, coordinate stabilizers, convex hulls,
projective angle schemes, Hayes monomial normalizers, and Carlitz-equivariant
enhancements are genuine new classification problems.  This is the useful
boundary: the tight-frame and conductor-sum theorem is already structural,
while the next layer asks how much of the integer cyclic geometry survives
when the torsion object is a non-cyclic Dedekind quotient, and where the
Dedekind side develops geometry of its own.
