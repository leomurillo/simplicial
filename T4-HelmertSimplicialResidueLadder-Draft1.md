## Abstract

**Situation.** The trilogy *Identifying 3* gave three layers of the same object. [\heb{ל}1](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20336872) supplied the native simplicial chart: keep the redundant coordinate, work in the mean-zero space, and read probability through the square-root sphere. [\heb{ל}2](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20338126) supplied the generating functional: the categorical log-partition \(\Phi=\log Z\), whose derivatives generate position, metric, cubic vertex, and the closure tower. [\heb{ל}3](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20339049) supplied the arithmetic substrate: when the outcome labels carry \(\mathbb Z/N\)-structure, the character basis decomposes the mean-zero space into conductor packets and the cubic vertex obeys the exact selection rule

\[
a+b+c\equiv 0 \pmod N.
\]

**Surprise.** After the trilogy, the dimensional ladder itself reveals another hidden structure. If one descends an \(N\)-slot simplicial ledger by arithmetic mean removal, then climbs back by appending the balancing coordinate, the process is lossless. The resulting coordinates are the unnormalized Helmert balances, with \(k\) leading ones followed by the balancing entry \(-k\):

\[
h_k=(1,\ldots,1,-k,0,\ldots,0),\qquad 1\le k\le N-1.
\]

This is the metric trick in ladder form: keep the integer incidence skeleton, and store length in the metric instead of leaking square roots into the coordinates.

**Action.** We formalize the reversible ladder, identify it with the unnormalized Helmert system, then deliberately remove the metric trick by orthonormalizing the ladder. The normalizers $\sqrt{k(k+1)}$ generate a multiquadratic field

\[
K_N=\mathbb Q\bigl(\sqrt{\operatorname{sf}(k(k+1))}:1\le k<N\bigr),
\]

where \(\operatorname{sf}\) denotes squarefree kernel.

**Result.** The field generated by the normalized ladder up to rung \(N\) is exactly

\[
K_N=\mathbb Q(\sqrt p:p\le N,\ p\text{ prime}).
\]

Hence the field grows precisely on prime rungs:

\[
K_N\ne K_{N-1}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad N\text{ is prime}.
\]

The statement is structural: primes are the rungs at which orthonormal measurement of the simplicial ladder forces a new quadratic square-root generator. The metric trick hides this arithmetic in the metric; unit normalization exposes it as radical residue.

**Harvest.** [\heb{ל}4](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20383504) therefore opens a fourth door after *Identifying 3*: the same mean-zero space that supports Fisher-Rao geometry, cumulant tensors, and conductor packets also supports a reversible balance ladder whose metric normalization externalizes number-theoretic square classes. The positive-cone version is the geometric-mean gauge, with multiplicative gauge removal corresponding to additive gauge removal under $\log$. This connects simplicial coordinates, Aitchison log-ratio geometry, Fisher-Rao information geometry, and the squarefree arithmetic of prime-generated quadratic fields.

**Keywords:** simplicial coordinates, quadrays, Helmert coordinates, mean-zero space, metric trick, squarefree kernel, multiquadratic fields, primorial residues, geometric mean, Aitchison geometry, Fisher-Rao geometry, conductor packets, information geometry.

## 0. The surprise after the trilogy

The trilogy was called *Identifying 3* because the number three appeared three times:

1. [\heb{ל}1](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20336872): for \(N=4\), the mean-zero simplicial space has dimension \(3\), so quadray coordinates carry ordinary 3D vector algebra.
2. [\heb{ל}2](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20338126): the third cumulant is the first information-geometric object beyond the Fisher-Rao metric.
3. [\heb{ל}3](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20339049): the cubic tensor is the first cumulant whose character-basis coefficients carry a modular selection rule.

[\heb{ל}4](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20383504) begins with another surprise of the same kind. The ladder that moves between \(N\)-slot simplicial ledgers and \((N-1)\)-slot ledgers is not merely a pedagogical device. It is a coordinate system already known in statistics under the Helmert name, but the usual presentation normalizes it immediately and thereby hides the integer skeleton.

The simplicial view asks us not to normalize too early.

Keep the redundancy. Center by arithmetic mean removal. Append the balancing coordinate when climbing back. The reward is a lossless dimensional ladder with integer rungs.

Then, only afterward, ask what happens when Euclidean unit measurement is imposed.

The answer: primes appear as new square-root field generators.

That is the paper.

## 1. The reversible ladder

Let

\[
q^{(N)}=(q_1,\ldots,q_N)\in\mathcal L_N.
\]

The descent step records the arithmetic mean

\[
m_N=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N q_i,
\]

centers the ledger

\[
r^{(N)}=C_N(q^{(N)})=q^{(N)}-m_N\mathbf 1_N,
\]

and rebalances downward by keeping the first \(N-1\) centered entries:

\[
q^{(N-1)}=(r_1^{(N)},\ldots,r_{N-1}^{(N)}).
\]

No information has been lost, because the missing entry is determined by the mean-zero condition:

\[
r_N^{(N)}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}r_i^{(N)}.
\]

Thus descent is reversible if the mean \(m_N\) is carried along.

Iterating downward gives a packet

\[
\{m_1,m_2,\ldots,m_N\}.
\]

The bottom coordinate \(m_1\) is the terminal one-slot ledger. It may be read as a mean or as a residual depending on orientation; both readings are useful. We keep the braces to mark the gauge-aware packet view.

The upward reconstruction is recursive:

\[
q^{(1)}=(m_1),
\]

and for \(2\le k\le N\),

\[
r^{(k)}=(q^{(k-1)},-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}q_i^{(k-1)}),
\]

\[
q^{(k)}=r^{(k)}+m_k\mathbf 1_k.
\]

This is centering and rebalancing, all the way down and all the way back up.

### Example 1.1

For

\[
q^{(4)}=(2,3,4,5),
\]

descent gives

\[
\{m_1,m_2,m_3,m_4\}
=
\left\{-\frac12,-\frac12,-\frac12,\frac72\right\}.
\]

Climbing back:

\[
q^{(1)}=\left(-\frac12\right),
\]

\[
q^{(2)}=\left(-\frac12,\frac12\right)-\frac12(1,1)=(-1,0),
\]

\[
q^{(3)}=(-1,0,1)-\frac12(1,1,1)=\left(-\frac32,-\frac12,\frac12\right),
\]

\[
q^{(4)}=\left(-\frac32,-\frac12,\frac12,\frac32\right)+\frac72(1,1,1,1)=(2,3,4,5).
\]

The ladder is lossless.

## 2. The metric trick is the unnormalized Helmert ladder

Define, for \(1\le k\le N-1\),

\[
h_k^{(N)}=(1,\ldots,1,-k,0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb R^N,
\]

with \(k\) leading ones. Also define

\[
h_N^{(N)}=\mathbf 1_N.
\]

These are the ladder vectors.

### Proposition 2.1. Orthogonality

For \(1\le j,k\le N-1\),

\[
h_j^{(N)}\cdot h_k^{(N)}=0\quad (j\ne k),
\]

and

\[
\|h_k^{(N)}\|^2=k(k+1).
\]

Also

\[
h_k^{(N)}\cdot\mathbf 1_N=0.
\]

**Proof.** Suppose \(j<k\). The first \(j\) entries of both vectors contribute \(j\). The \((j+1)\)-st entry contributes \((-j)(1)=-j\), because \(h_k^{(N)}\) still has value \(1\) there. All later entries contribute zero until the \((k+1)\)-st entry, where \(h_j^{(N)}\) is already zero. Hence the dot product is \(j-j=0\). The norm is

\[
\|h_k^{(N)}\|^2=k\cdot 1^2+(-k)^2=k+k^2=k(k+1).
\]

The sum of entries is \(k-k=0\), so \(h_k^{(N)}\cdot\mathbf 1_N=0\). \(\square\)

### Proposition 2.2. Direct reconstruction

Every \(q\in\mathbb R^N\) has the expansion

\[
q=m_N\mathbf 1_N+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}m_k h_k^{(N)},
\]

where

\[
m_N=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N q_i,
\]

and

\[
m_k=\frac{h_k^{(N)}\cdot q}{k(k+1)}
=
\frac{q_1+\cdots+q_k-kq_{k+1}}{k(k+1)}.
\]

**Proof.** The vectors \(h_1^{(N)},\ldots,h_{N-1}^{(N)}\) are mutually orthogonal and span the mean-zero space \(H_N\), while \(\mathbf 1_N\) spans the shared-displacement line. Since they are mutually orthogonal and non-zero, the coefficient formulas are the usual orthogonal projection formulas. \(\square\)

This is the clean algebraic heart of [\heb{ל}4](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20383504).

The metric trick says: do not divide by \(\sqrt{k(k+1)}\) unless unit measurement is actually needed. Keep \(h_k\) integer, and carry the metric.

The exact-arithmetic metric from the quadray engineering guide generalizes as

\[
M_N=NI_N-J_N.
\]

On the mean-zero space \(H_N\), this reduces to

\[
u^T M_N v=N\,u\cdot v.
\]

So in the ladder basis, the metric is diagonal:

\[
h_j^T M_N h_k=N k(k+1)\delta_{jk}.
\]

The integer skeleton is simple; the metric remembers the lengths.

## 3. First divergence: the unit ladder and the quadratic field clock

Now deliberately remove the metric trick.

Define the unit Helmert vectors

\[
e_k^{(N)}=\frac{h_k^{(N)}}{\sqrt{k(k+1)}}.
\]

The square roots

\[
\sqrt{k(k+1)}
\]

are the normalization cost of forcing the integer ladder into Euclidean unit form.

Let

\[
K_N=\mathbb Q\bigl(\sqrt{\operatorname{sf}(k(k+1))}:1\le k<N\bigr),
\]

where \(\operatorname{sf}(n)\) is the squarefree kernel of \(n\).

### Theorem 3.1. Prime rungs are field-extension rungs

For every \(N\ge2\),

\[
K_N=\mathbb Q(\sqrt p:p\le N,\ p\text{ prime}).
\]

Consequently,

\[
K_N\ne K_{N-1}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
N\text{ is prime}.
\]

More precisely, when \(N\) is prime,

\[
[K_N:K_{N-1}]=2,
\]

and when \(N\) is composite,

\[
K_N=K_{N-1}.
\]

**Proof.** Every prime factor of \(k(k+1)\) with \(1\le k<N\) is at most \(N\), so

\[
K_N\subseteq\mathbb Q(\sqrt p:p\le N,\ p\text{ prime}).
\]

Conversely, let \(p\le N\) be prime. For \(p=2\), the rung \(k=1\) contributes \(\sqrt{1\cdot2}=\sqrt2\). For \(p>2\), take \(k=p-1\). The normalizer is

\[
\sqrt{(p-1)p}.
\]

All prime factors of \(p-1\) are smaller than \(p\), hence their square roots have already appeared by induction. Therefore \(\sqrt p\) lies in \(K_N\). This proves equality.

The second claim follows immediately: passing from \(N-1\) to \(N\) adds \(\sqrt N\) exactly when \(N\) is prime. The square classes of distinct primes are independent in \(\mathbb Q^\times/(\mathbb Q^\times)^2\), so adjoining \(\sqrt N\) for prime \(N\) doubles the degree. \(\square\)

This theorem should not be oversold as a prime algorithm. To decide field growth efficiently one still needs access to squarefree kernels, which is already factorization-shaped information. The theorem is structural:

\[
\boxed{\text{primes are the rungs where unit measurement requires a new quadratic generator}.}
\]

### Examples

For \(N=4\):

\[
K_4=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3).
\]

The rung \(4\) is composite, so no new generator appears:

\[
\sqrt{3\cdot4}=\sqrt{12}=2\sqrt3.
\]

For \(N=5\):

\[
\sqrt{4\cdot5}=2\sqrt5,
\]

so

\[
K_5=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt5).
\]

A new prime rung has arrived.

For \(N=6\):

\[
\sqrt{5\cdot6}=\sqrt{30}=\sqrt2\sqrt3\sqrt5,
\]

so the field does not grow.

For \(N=7\):

\[
\sqrt{6\cdot7}=\sqrt{42}=\sqrt2\sqrt3\sqrt7,
\]

and \(\sqrt7\) is new. The field grows again.

The radicals observed in explicit unit-frame computations,

\[
\sqrt7,
\sqrt{14},
\sqrt{21},
\sqrt{42},
\sqrt{70},
\sqrt{105},
\sqrt{210},
\]

are therefore not accidents. They are visible elements of the new \(\sqrt7\)-coset inside the multiquadratic field generated by primes up to \(7\).

## 4. Second divergence: multiplicative gauge and geometric-mean balances

The ladder has an additive form and a multiplicative form.

Let

\[
x=(x_1,\ldots,x_N)\in\mathbb R_{>0}^N.
\]

The shared multiplicative magnitude is the geometric mean

\[
g(x)=\left(\prod_{i=1}^N x_i\right)^{1/N}.
\]

Multiplicative gauge removal is

\[
x_i\mapsto \frac{x_i}{g(x)},
\]

and it lands in the product-one space

\[
\prod_{i=1}^N \frac{x_i}{g(x)}=1.
\]

Taking logs turns this into arithmetic mean removal. Let

\[
y_i=\log x_i.
\]

Then

\[
\log\frac{x_i}{g(x)}
=y_i-\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^N y_j.
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{
\text{multiplicative gauge removal}
\quad\xleftrightarrow{\log}\quad
\text{additive gauge removal}.
}
\]

The Helmert ladder now becomes a ladder of geometric-mean comparisons. Compute

\[
h_k\cdot y
=\sum_{i=1}^k \log x_i-k\log x_{k+1}
=\log\frac{x_1x_2\cdots x_k}{x_{k+1}^k}.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
h_k\cdot y
=k\log\frac{\operatorname{GM}(x_1,\ldots,x_k)}{x_{k+1}}.
\]

The unit version is

\[
e_k\cdot y
=\sqrt{\frac{k}{k+1}}
\log\frac{\operatorname{GM}(x_1,\ldots,x_k)}{x_{k+1}}.
\]

This is precisely the form of a sequential log-ratio balance: compare the geometric mean of the previous block against the next part.

Thus the same ladder has three readings:

\[
\text{additive ledger: arithmetic mean removal},
\]

\[
\text{positive cone: geometric mean removal},
\]

\[
\text{log space: Helmert/Aitchison balances}.
\]

This is the bridge to information geometry. [\heb{ל}2](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20338126)'s softmax is

\[
p_i=\frac{e^{\eta_i}}{\sum_j e^{\eta_j}}.
\]

The additive gauge

\[
\eta\mapsto\eta+c\mathbf1
\]

becomes multiplicative scaling of positive weights

\[
e^{\eta_i}\mapsto e^c e^{\eta_i}.
\]

Softmax removes that multiplicative gauge by sum-normalization. The geometric-mean gauge removes it by product-normalization. Both live on the same positive cone; they choose different sections.

[\heb{ל}1](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20336872) emphasized the square-root section

\[
p_i\mapsto a_i=\sqrt{p_i},
\]

which turns probability into sphere geometry. [\heb{ל}4](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20383504) adds the log-ratio section

\[
x_i\mapsto \log x_i-\frac1N\sum_j\log x_j,
\]

which turns positive composition into mean-zero balance geometry.

These are not rival geometries. They are two observer choices on positive measure.

## 5. Third divergence: conductor packets and radical residues

[\heb{ל}3](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20339049) showed that when labels carry \(\mathbb Z/N\)-structure, the complexified mean-zero space decomposes into additive conductor packets

\[
H_N\otimes\mathbb C=\bigoplus_{d\mid N,\ d>1}H_d.
\]

The packet \(H_d\) has dimension

\[
\dim_{\mathbb C}H_d=\varphi(d),
\]

and the cubic vertex at the uniform obeys

\[
T(\chi_a,\chi_b,\chi_c)=1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 a+b+c\equiv0\pmod N
\]

for non-trivial characters.

That is the arithmetic of cyclic labels.

[\heb{ל}4](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20383504) finds a different arithmetic in the same mean-zero space: the arithmetic of metric normalization. The normalized ladder lives in the multiquadratic field

\[
K_N=\mathbb Q(\sqrt p:p\le N,\ p\text{ prime}).
\]

A natural basis for this field over \(\mathbb Q\) is

\[
\{\sqrt d:d\mid P_N,\ d\text{ squarefree}\},
\]

where

\[
P_N=\prod_{p\le N}p
\]

is the primorial over primes up to \(N\).

Thus the divisor lattice appears twice:

1. In [\heb{ל}3](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20339049), divisors \(d\mid N\) classify character conductors of \(\mathbb Z/N\).
2. In [\heb{ל}4](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20383504), squarefree divisors \(d\mid P_N\) classify radical basis elements of the normalized ladder field.

These are not the same decomposition. One is Fourier-arithmetic inside a fixed cyclic group. The other is metric-arithmetic accumulated by climbing the dimensional ladder.

But they rhyme.

[\heb{ל}3](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20339049) says:

\[
\text{order 3 detects the additive ring structure of }\mathbb Z/N.
\]

[\heb{ל}4](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20383504) says:

\[
\text{unit measurement detects the prime square classes accumulated up to }N.
\]

The first is a selection rule. The second is a field-extension rule.

Together they suggest a larger arithmetic picture:

\[
\text{mean-zero simplicial geometry}
\quad
\begin{cases}
\text{Fourier basis} & \to \text{conductor packets},\\
\text{Helmert ladder} & \to \text{quadratic radical packets}.
\end{cases}
\]

This is not yet a theorem connecting the two packet systems. It is a map of the terrain.

## 6. Fourth divergence: the value clock

The prime field of \S3 entered through the *dimensions* of the ladder: the unit normalizers \(\sqrt{k(k+1)}\) accumulate the primes \(p\le N\), independent of what the slots actually carry. There is a second, logically independent way primes enter the same mean-zero space \(H_N\) — through the *values* in the slots, once the ledger is read as amplitudes in the square-root section of [\heb{ל}1](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20336872).

Let \(q\in\mathbb Z_{>0}^N\) with total \(S=\sum_i q_i\). The probability section is \(p=q/S\), and the amplitude (square-root sphere) section is

\[
a_i=\sqrt{p_i}=\frac{\sqrt{q_i}}{\sqrt S}=\frac{\sqrt{q_i S}}{S}.
\]

### Definition 6.1. The amplitude field

The amplitude field of the data \(q\) is

\[
\mathcal A(q):=\mathbb Q(a_1,\ldots,a_N)
=\mathbb Q\bigl(\sqrt{\operatorname{sf}(q_i S)}:1\le i\le N\bigr),
\]

the field generated over \(\mathbb Q\) by the amplitude coordinates.

The two descriptions agree because \(a_i=\sqrt{q_iS}/S\) and \(S\in\mathbb Q\), so adjoining \(a_i\) is the same as adjoining \(\sqrt{q_iS}\), whose value depends only on the squarefree kernel \(\operatorname{sf}(q_iS)\).

### Theorem 6.2. The value clock

Let \(q\in\mathbb Z_{>0}^N\) with \(S=\sum_i q_i\). Then \(\mathcal A(q)\) is the multiquadratic field

\[
\mathcal A(q)=\mathbb Q\bigl(\sqrt d:[d]\in V_q\bigr),
\qquad
V_q=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb F_2}\bigl\{[q_iS]:1\le i\le N\bigr\}
\subseteq \mathbb Q^\times/(\mathbb Q^\times)^2,
\]

with Galois group \((\mathbb Z/2)^{\dim_{\mathbb F_2}V_q}\) and

\[
\mathcal A(q)\subseteq\mathbb Q\bigl(\sqrt p:p\mid q_1\cdots q_N\,S\bigr).
\]

In particular the value clock adjoins exactly the square classes spanned by the \([q_iS]\), and these may involve primes absent from the dimensional range \(\{p\le N\}\): both the primes carried as data *values* and the primes dividing the *total* \(S\).

**Proof.** By Definition 6.1, \(\mathcal A(q)\) is generated by the radicals \(\sqrt{q_iS}\), and \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt{m},\sqrt{n})=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{m},\sqrt{mn})\) shows the generated field depends only on the \(\mathbb F_2\)-span of the square classes \([q_iS]\). A compositum of quadratic fields indexed by an \(\mathbb F_2\)-basis of \(V_q\) is multiquadratic with Galois group \((\mathbb Z/2)^{\dim V_q}\). Every prime ramifying in \(\mathcal A(q)\) divides some \(q_iS\), giving the stated containment. \(\square\)

The dimensional clock of \S3 and the value clock of \S6 run at different rates on the same data.

### Example 6.3. \(q=(2,3,5)\)

Here \(S=10\), so \(q_iS=20,30,50\) with squarefree kernels \(5,30,2\). Thus

\[
\mathcal A(2,3,5)=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt5,\sqrt{30})=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt5)=K_5,
\]

degree \(8\). The dimensional clock at \(N=3\) gave only \(K_3=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)\). The value clock additionally exposes \(\sqrt5\), the prime carried in the third slot. The amplitude vector is the prime-radical point

\[
a=\frac1{\sqrt{10}}(\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt5)
=\left(\tfrac{\sqrt5}{5},\tfrac{\sqrt{30}}{10},\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right).
\]

### Example 6.4. \(q=(2,3,5,7)\)

Now \(S=17\) is prime, and \(q_iS=34,51,85,119\) are all squarefree, so

\[
\mathcal A(2,3,5,7)=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{34},\sqrt{51},\sqrt{85},\sqrt{119}),
\]

degree \(16\). In the prime basis \((2,3,5,7,17)\) the square classes are

\[
[34]=(1,0,0,0,1),\ [51]=(0,1,0,0,1),\ [85]=(0,0,1,0,1),\ [119]=(0,0,0,1,1),
\]

spanning the index-\(2\) subspace \(\{(a,b,c,d,e):e\equiv a+b+c+d\bmod2\}\). Hence \(\sqrt{17}=(0,0,0,0,1)\) is **not** in \(\mathcal A\): adjoining it would force \(1\equiv0\), doubling the degree to \(32\). The value clock therefore sees the prime set \(\{2,3,5,7,17\}\) but only through the *even* subfield of \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt5,\sqrt7,\sqrt{17})\). Meanwhile the dimensional clock is flat here: \(N=4\) is composite (rung \(k=3\) gives \(\sqrt{12}=2\sqrt3\)), so it still sees only \(\{2,3\}\).

### Remark 6.5. The total as a hidden prime injector

Example 6.4 shows the role of the total \(S\). When \(S\) is prime, or has large prime factors, every amplitude class is twisted by \(\sqrt S\), so the value clock can adjoin a prime that is neither a slot index nor a slot value. This is the amplitude-reading analogue of the conductor in [\heb{ל}3](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20339049): there the modulus \(N\) structures the Fourier packets; here the total \(S\) structures the radical square classes. The constraint \(e\equiv a+b+c+d\) is exactly the statement that every generator carries one factor of \(S\).

### Proposition 6.6. The sextic radical: the multiplicative-amplitude corner

Compose the multiplicative gauge removal of \S4 with the amplitude reading. With \(x\in\mathbb R_{>0}^N\) and geometric mean \(g=(\prod_i x_i)^{1/N}\), set \(\tilde a_i=\sqrt{x_i/g}\). Then

\[
\tilde a_i=\frac{\sqrt{x_i}}{g^{1/2}},
\qquad
g^{1/2}=\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^N x_i\Bigr)^{1/(2N)},
\]

so the only non-quadratic generator adjoined is the \(2N\)-th root of the data product. For \(x=(2,3,5)\), with product \(P=30\) and \(2N=6\),

\[
\mathbb Q\bigl(\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt5,\,30^{1/6}\bigr)=K_5\bigl(30^{1/6}\bigr),
\qquad
[\,K_5(30^{1/6}):\mathbb Q\,]=8\cdot3=24,
\]

a genuine cubic extension of \(K_5\). For first-prime data this radical is the \(2N\)-th root of the primorial.

**Proof.** \(x_i/g=x_i\,(\prod_j x_j)^{-1/N}\), so \(\sqrt{x_i/g}=\sqrt{x_i}\,(\prod_j x_j)^{-1/(2N)}\); the factors \(\sqrt{x_i}\) lie in a multiquadratic field, and the common factor \(g^{1/2}=(\prod_j x_j)^{1/(2N)}\) is the single new generator. For \((2,3,5)\), \(K_5\) already contains \(\sqrt{30}=(30^{1/6})^3\), so \(30^{1/6}\) is a root of \(x^3-\sqrt{30}\), irreducible over \(K_5\) because \(\sqrt{30}\) is not a cube in the multiquadratic field \(K_5\). The degree is \(8\cdot3=24\). \(\square\)

### Remark 6.7. The eight corners

The three switches — additive vs multiplicative gauge, metric trick on vs off, probability vs amplitude — generate a cube. Only the amplitude reading produces *value*-dependent radicals; only the multiplicative gauge produces a \(2N\)-th root. Their interaction is summarized below.

| Gauge | Metric | Reading | Object for \((2,3,5)\) | Arithmetic exposed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| additive | trick on | probability | \((-\tfrac12,-\tfrac56)\) in \(M_3\) | none (integer skeleton) |
| additive | trick off | probability | \(-\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2},-\tfrac{5\sqrt6}{6}\) | dimensional \(K_3=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)\) |
| additive | trick on | amplitude | Helmert coeffs of \(a\) over \(M_3\) | value \(K_5\) in coeffs |
| additive | trick off | amplitude | \(\frac1{\sqrt{10}}(\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt5)\) | value \(K_5=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt5)\) |
| multiplicative | trick on | probability | \(\log\tfrac23,\log\tfrac6{25}\) | prime-ratio balances |
| multiplicative | trick off | probability | \(\tfrac{\log(2/3)}{\sqrt2},\tfrac{\log(6/25)}{\sqrt6}\) | balances \(\times\) dimensional radicals |
| multiplicative | trick off | amplitude | \(\sqrt{x_i}/30^{1/6}\) | sextic \(K_5(30^{1/6})\), degree \(24\) |

The additive face stays quadratic and splits into a dimensional clock (\S3) and a value clock (\S6); the multiplicative-amplitude corner alone escapes into a \(2N\)-th-radical extension. The fine radical support inside each multiquadratic field is the value-clock instance of Forward Problem 8.5.

## 8. Theorems and forward program

The results above opened several doors. Some are no longer conjectural once the gauge-corridor and Fourier-Mellin viewpoint are made explicit. We separate the theorem-level content from the remaining forward problems.

### Theorem 8.1. Gauge sections of the positive projective cone

Let

\[
\mathbb P_+^{N-1}:=\mathbb R_{>0}^N/\mathbb R_{>0}^{\times}
\]

be the positive projective cone. The sum-one simplex section

\[
\sigma_1([x])=\frac{x}{\sum_i x_i}
\]

and the product-one section

\[
\sigma_0([x])=\frac{x}{G(x)},\qquad
G(x)=\left(\prod_i x_i\right)^{1/N},
\]

are well-defined global gauge sections of \(\mathbb P_+^{N-1}\). More generally, the power-mean gauges

\[
\sigma_r([x])=\frac{x}{M_r(x)}
\]

for \(r\in\mathbb R\cup\{\pm\infty\}\), with \(r=0\) interpreted as the geometric mean, select one representative from each positive ray whenever the corresponding mean is finite.

**Proof.** If \(x\sim \lambda x\) with \(\lambda>0\), then \(\sum_i(\lambda x_i)=\lambda\sum_i x_i\) and \(G(\lambda x)=\lambda G(x)\), so both displayed formulas are independent of the chosen representative. Each positive ray meets the sum-one section exactly once, at \(x/\sum_i x_i\), and meets the product-one section exactly once, at \(x/G(x)\). The same homogeneity argument applies to every power mean: \(M_r(\lambda x)=\lambda M_r(x)\). \(\square\)

Thus the square-root map of [\heb{ל}1](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20336872) and the log-ratio map of [\heb{ל}4](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20383504) are not rival geometries. They are two gauge choices on the same positive projective object.

### Theorem 8.2. Helmert-Fourier coefficient factorization

Identify the outcome labels with \(\mathbb Z/N=\{0,\ldots,N-1\}\), let

\[
\omega=e^{2\pi i/N},\qquad \chi_c(s)=\omega^{cs},
\]

and let \(h_k\) have \(k\) leading ones, entry \(-k\) at slot \(k\), and zeros afterward. Define the normalized Helmert-Fourier coefficient

\[
U_{kc}
:=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N\,k(k+1)}}\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}h_k(s)\chi_c(s),
\qquad
1\le k\le N-1.
\]

For \(c\ne0\),

\[
U_{kc}
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N\,k(k+1)}}
\left(
\frac{1-\omega^{kc}}{1-\omega^c}
-k\omega^{kc}
\right).
\]

The prefactor is the radical Helmert factor; the bracketed term is the cyclotomic conductor factor. For \(c=0\), \(U_{k0}=0\). Hence every transfer coefficient lies in the compositum

\[
K_N\,\mathbb Q(\zeta_N),
\]

where \(K_N=\mathbb Q(\sqrt p:p\le N,\ p\text{ prime})\).

**Proof.** By definition,

\[
\sum_s h_k(s)\chi_c(s)
=\sum_{s=0}^{k-1}\omega^{cs}-k\omega^{kc}.
\]

For \(c\ne0\), the first term is the geometric sum

\[
\sum_{s=0}^{k-1}\omega^{cs}
=\frac{1-\omega^{kc}}{1-\omega^c}.
\]

For \(c=0\), the sum is \(k-k=0\). The normalization factor belongs to \(K_N\): its squarefree part uses only primes dividing \(N k(k+1)\), all of which are at most \(N\). The bracketed term belongs to \(\mathbb Q(\zeta_N)\). \(\square\)

This proves the entry-level conductor-residue dictionary: a Helmert-Fourier transfer coefficient is radical arithmetic from the dimensional ladder times cyclotomic arithmetic from the conductor basis.

### Remark 8.2A. A worked transfer: $(2,3,5)$ on $H_4$

The radical-times-cyclotomic factorization of Theorem 8.2 is legible on a single vector. Read $(2,3,5)$ as Helmert coordinates; the vector they build on $H_4$ (the lift of \S1, $\mathbb R^3\cong H_4$) is

\[
w=2h_1+3h_2+5h_3=(10,6,-1,-15),\qquad \textstyle\sum_i w_i=0,
\]

and its two frame-readings are

\[
\text{Helmert }(2,3,5)
\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{Fourier }\bigl(\tfrac{11}4-\tfrac{21}4 i,\ \tfrac92,\ \tfrac{11}4+\tfrac{21}4 i\bigr),
\]

the Fourier reading being the $\mathbb Z/4$ characters of $w$: the DC slot $10=\sum_i w_i$ is the shared "mass," the $\chi_1,\chi_3$ conjugate pair is the shape, and $\chi_2$ (Nyquist) is real. The bare coordinate transfer carries one reading to the other exactly,

\[
T\,(2,3,5)^{\!\top}=\bigl(\tfrac{11}4-\tfrac{21}4 i,\ \tfrac92,\ \tfrac{11}4+\tfrac{21}4 i\bigr)^{\!\top},
\qquad
T=\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac{1+i}4 & \tfrac{3-i}4 & -i\\[3pt] \tfrac12 & -\tfrac12 & 1\\[3pt] \tfrac{1-i}4 & \tfrac{3+i}4 & i\end{pmatrix}\in M_3\bigl(\mathbb Q(i)\bigr),
\]

purely cyclotomic, with rows $1$ and $3$ conjugate. The **$K_4$ radicals appear precisely on passing to the orthonormal apex frame**: restoring the Helmert normalizers $\sqrt{k(k+1)}$ gives the unitary $U_4$ of Theorem 8.2, with for instance

\[
U_{11}=\frac{1-i}{2\sqrt2}\ \ (\text{the }\sqrt2\text{ rung}),\qquad
U_{31}=\frac{i}{\sqrt3}\ \ (\text{the }\sqrt3\text{ rung}),
\]

every entry lying in $K_4\,\mathbb Q(\zeta_4)=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3,i)$, and $U_4U_4^{*}=I$ (verified to $4\times10^{-16}$). Thus $(2,3,5)$ separates the two factors of a transfer coefficient cleanly: the **cyclotomic conductor part is the bare transfer $T$**, and the **radical metric part (\S3) is exactly the normalization cost**. This $3\times3$ is the apex unitary $U_N$ at $N=4$ — the Helmert–Fourier transfer of Theorem 8.2 — whose $N\to\infty$ scaling limit is the archimedean operator of the Numbers series; the worked transfer is one finite rung of that ladder. (Certified `empirical/probe_helmert_lift_ladder.py`, `empirical/probe_apex_U4_closes_loop.py`.)

### Theorem 8.3. Cubic Helmert selection rule

At the uniform distribution, the categorical third cumulant contracted against mean-zero vectors is

\[
T_u(v,w,z)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N v_iw_iz_i.
\]

For unnormalized Helmert vectors, sort the indices \(a\le b\le c\). Then

\[
T_u(h_a,h_b,h_c)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{a(a+1)}{N}, & a=b<c,\\[6pt]
\dfrac{a-a^3}{N}, & a=b=c,\\[6pt]
0, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]

**Proof.** If \(a<b\le c\), the first \(a\) entries contribute \(a\), while the balancing entry of \(h_a\) contributes \(-a\); all later entries vanish because \(h_a\) is zero there. Hence the total is \(0\). If \(a=b<c\), the first \(a\) entries contribute \(a\), and the balancing entry contributes \((-a)(-a)(1)=a^2\), giving \(a(a+1)\). If \(a=b=c\), the first \(a\) entries contribute \(a\), and the balancing entry contributes \((-a)^3=-a^3\). Multiplying by \(1/N\) gives the displayed formula. \(\square\)

This is the real Helmert-basis analogue of [\heb{ל}3](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20339049)'s modular cubic selection rule. The Fourier basis expresses the rule as character conservation; the Helmert basis expresses it as triangular balance incidence.

### Remark 8.4. No algorithmic speed claim

The field-growth criterion

\[
K_N\ne K_{N-1}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad N\text{ is prime}
\]

is structural, not a faster prime sieve. Recognizing the new square class requires squarefree-kernel information, which is already factorization-shaped information. What the theorem supplies is a representation: prime rungs are the places where unit measurement forces a new quadratic generator.

### Forward Problem 8.5. Fine radical support of canonical ladder lifts

Theorem 3.1 gives the invariant field \(K_N\). A finer question remains: for a fixed recursive unit-frame convention and a chosen ladder vector, which square classes inside \(K_N\) actually appear?

For canonical first-axis ladder packets, examples suggest that the radical support appearing at a prime rung \(p\) lies in the new coset

\[
\sqrt p\,K_{p-1}\subset K_p,
\]

while composite rungs recombine previous square classes. This is observed in examples such as

\[
N=5:\quad \sqrt5,\sqrt{10},\sqrt{30},
\]

and

\[
N=7:\quad
\sqrt7,\sqrt{14},\sqrt{21},\sqrt{42},\sqrt{70},\sqrt{105},\sqrt{210}.
\]

Because the exact support depends on the recursive convention and on the chosen ladder vector, this is a support-classification problem rather than another field-growth theorem.

### Forward Problem 8.6. Packet-level conductor-residue dictionary

Theorem 8.2 proves factorization entry by entry. The remaining dictionary is packet-level: describe the full Helmert-Fourier transfer matrix as a structured object over conductor packets, ideally as a Gauss-sum / Vandermonde / discrete-Fourier hybrid with explicit block behavior.

This is where the two divisor lattices should meet:

1. divisors \(d\mid N\) controlling additive conductor packets in [\heb{ל}3](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20339049);
2. squarefree divisors \(d\mid P_N\) controlling radical basis elements in \(K_N\).

The dictionary is not equality. It should relate two ways arithmetic enters \(H_N\): through cyclic label structure at fixed \(N\), and through metric normalization across the Helmert flag

\[
H_2\subset H_3\subset\cdots\subset H_N.
\]

### Forward Problem 8.7. Higher cumulant balances in Helmert coordinates

Theorem 8.3 settles the cubic order. Higher categorical cumulants of [\heb{ל}2](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20338126), expressed in unnormalized Helmert coordinates, should admit closed triangular formulas whose coefficients separate:

1. integer incidence data from the ladder;
2. metric normalization data from the choice of unit basis.

From order \(4\) onward the connected cumulant formulas require partition-subtraction terms, so the higher-order Helmert selection rules should be stated for connected cumulants rather than raw powers.

## 10. Relation to known names

This paper deliberately uses the language of simplicial ledgers and gauge because the conceptual work began from quadrays and the dimensional ladder. Still, several standard mathematical objects are present.

1. The vectors \(h_k\) are the unnormalized Helmert contrast vectors.
2. The unit vectors \(e_k\) are the Helmert orthonormal basis.
3. The log-positive version is a sequential log-ratio balance from compositional data analysis.
4. The product-one section is the centered log-ratio gauge of Aitchison geometry.
5. The mean-zero space \(H_N\) is the real standard representation of the symmetric group \(S_N\).
6. The integer mean-zero points form the root lattice \(A_{N-1}\), though the Helmert ladder basis is an orthogonal rational basis rather than the usual simple-root basis.
7. The fields \(K_N\) are multiquadratic extensions with Galois group

\[
\operatorname{Gal}(K_N/\mathbb Q)\cong(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^{\pi(N)}.
\]

The novelty is not that these objects were unknown. The novelty is their alignment inside one simplicial ladder:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{quadray intuition}
&\to \text{mean-zero balance}
\to \text{metric trick}\\
&\to \text{prime field residues}
\to \text{information geometry}.
\end{aligned}
\]

[\heb{ל}1](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20336872) said: do not delete the redundant coordinate.

[\heb{ל}2](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20338126) said: one scalar functional generates the tower.

[\heb{ל}3](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20339049) said: the cubic vertex is where arithmetic first speaks.

[\heb{ל}4](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20383504) says: do not normalize the ladder too early.

If we keep the integer ladder and carry the metric, the simplicial system remains clean:

\[
\{1,0,\ldots,0\}\mapsto(1,-1,0,\ldots,0).
\]

If we insist on unit Euclidean measurement, the hidden arithmetic appears:

\[
\sqrt{k(k+1)},
\qquad
\mathbb Q(\sqrt p:p\le N,\ p\text{ prime}).
\]

So the same object has two faces:

\[
\text{integer balance skeleton}
\]

and

\[
\text{quadratic prime-residue measurement field}.
\]

This is the post-trilogy surprise. The dimensional ladder is not only the path from simplicial \(N\) to Cartesian \(N-1\). It is also a gauge machine, a log-ratio machine, and a prime-field clock.

The next work is to decide how much of this is merely beautiful language, and how much can be made into new mathematics.

[\heb{ל}4](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20383504) opens that door.
