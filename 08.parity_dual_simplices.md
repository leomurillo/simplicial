## Abstract

This paper gives a spectral generalization of the \(N=4\) stella-octangula phenomenon to all even \(N\).  The governing operation is not the Galois conjugation permutation \(v_i\mapsto v_{-i}\), but the \(\Phi_2\) cyclotomic projector.  For even \(N\), the cyclic shift \(C_N\) on the zero-sum hyperplane

$$
H_N=\{x\in\mathbb R^N:\sum_i x_i=0\}
$$

has a one-dimensional parity eigenspace

$$
H_N^{[2]}=\ker(C_N+I).
$$

Reflecting the regular simplex frame across the hyperplane perpendicular to this parity axis produces a second regular simplex frame.  This is the **parity-dual simplex**.  For \(N=4\), the construction recovers the antipodal/complementary tetrahedron of the stella octangula, up to a cyclic relabeling.  That stella-octangula coincidence is special to \(N=4\).  For even \(N\geq6\), the parity-dual simplex is canonical but is no longer the antipodal simplex.  For odd \(N\), no \(\Phi_2\) component exists, so this particular canonical parity dual does not exist, although central inversion still produces an ordinary polar simplex in every dimension.

The first part of the paper gives the construction, its cross-Gram matrix, its \(N=4\) specialization, its parity-class stabilizer, and the prism/free-sum structure of the convex hull.  Throughout, "ETF" refers to each regular simplex frame separately: the union \(V_N\cup W_N\) is a compound of two simplex ETFs, not in general a single equiangular frame.

The second part replaces the single parity reflection by all cyclotomic block-sign reflections.  The resulting spectral compound \(X_N^{\mathrm{spec}}\) is a centrally symmetric unit-norm tight frame whose non-antipodal projective angles are exactly cyclotomic traces, equivalently Ramanujan sums divided by \(N-1\).  A projective packing table places these frames against the Welch and Levenshtein baselines: prime cases are the expected antipodal simplex ETFs, while the small composite cases pay a visible coherence cost for their arithmetic structure.  Prime spectral hulls, prime-power coordinate stabilizers, the even semiprime family \(N=2p\), and the odd squarefree two-prime family \(N=pq\) admit complete structural theorems.  The coordinate-pair colors are shown to be fusions of the divisor association scheme on \(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z\), and the first mixed-composite rigidity case \(N=12\) is proved.  The \(N=12\) Fourier refinement also separates coordinate-permutation rigidity from reflection-refinement rigidity: divisor-lattice coupling at the coordinate level does not force cross-block coupling among the conjugate Fourier planes.  Mixed-composite face lattices, Fourier-plane refinements, and full orthogonal stabilizers beyond these cases are deliberately separated as conjectural and computational territory.

**Keywords:** regular simplex, zero-sum hyperplane, cyclotomic decomposition, parity reflection, stella octangula, Ramanujan sums, equiangular tight frames, Grassmannian frames, simplex compounds, spectral compounds, association schemes.

## 1. Setup

Index coordinates modulo \(N\), and set

$$
v_i=e_i-\frac1N\mathbf 1,\qquad i\in\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z.
$$

The \(v_i\) span

$$
H_N=\mathbf 1^\perp\subset\mathbb R^N
$$

and satisfy

$$
\sum_i v_i=0,
\qquad
\langle v_i,v_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}-\frac1N.
$$

After unit normalization, they form the regular simplex ETF in \(H_N\).  Let

$$
C_Nv_i=v_{i+1}
$$

be the cyclic shift.

Historically, the motivating \(N=4\) orientation bit came from Andy's quadray-rotor formulation, where choosing one of the two complementary tetrahedra in the stella octangula fixes the sign of the \(H_4\) volume form and hence the handedness convention for cross-product/rotor formulas.  This note does not use that terminology as a formal primitive, but Section 4 returns to it after the spectral identity is available.  The intrinsic object studied here is the **parity-dual simplex** produced by the \(\Phi_2\) spectral reflection.

For even \(N\), define the alternating parity vector

$$
s=(1,-1,1,-1,\ldots,1,-1)^T.
$$

Then

$$
\sum_i s_i=0,
$$

so \(s\in H_N\), and

$$
C_Ns=-s.
$$

Thus \(s\) spans the \(\Phi_2\) cyclotomic component:

$$
H_N^{[2]}=\ker(C_N+I)=\mathbb Rs.
$$

For odd \(N\), \(C_N\) has no eigenvalue \(-1\), and this parity component is absent.

## 2. The Parity Reflection

For even \(N\), let

$$
P_2=\frac1Nss^T
$$

be the orthogonal projector onto \(H_N^{[2]}\).  Define

$$
\rho_2=I-2P_2.
$$

Then \(\rho_2\) is the reflection that flips the parity axis and fixes its orthogonal complement:

$$
\rho_2s=-s,
\qquad
\rho_2|_{s^\perp\cap H_N}=I.
$$

Since \(P_2\) is the spectral projector for the \(-1\)-eigenspace of \(C_N\), it commutes with \(C_N\).  Hence

$$
\rho_2C_N=C_N\rho_2.
$$

It also commutes with the Galois-simplex relabelings \(\sigma_a(v_i)=v_{ai}\) for \(a\in(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^\times\).  Indeed, when \(N\) is even every unit \(a\) is odd, so \((-1)^{ai}=(-1)^i\), and \(\sigma_a\) fixes the parity axis \(\mathbb Rs\).

The determinant of \(\rho_2|_{H_N}\) is \(-1\).  It is a canonical orientation-reversing isometry of \(H_N\) when \(N\) is even.

## 3. The Parity-Dual Simplex

Define the parity-dual vertices

$$
w_i=\rho_2v_i.
$$

Since \(\rho_2\) is an orthogonal transformation of \(H_N\), the \(w_i\) form a second regular simplex ETF:

$$
\sum_iw_i=0,
\qquad
\langle w_i,w_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}-\frac1N.
$$

The explicit formula is

$$
w_i
=
v_i-\frac{2s_i}{N}s.
$$

### Theorem 3.1: Parity-Dual Simplex

For even \(N\), the frame

$$
W_N=\{w_i=\rho_2v_i\}_{i=0}^{N-1}
$$

is a canonically defined regular simplex ETF in \(H_N\).  It is determined only by the cyclic structure and the \(\Phi_2\) component.  The cross-Gram matrix between the original simplex \(V_N=\{v_i\}\) and its parity dual \(W_N\) is

$$
\langle v_i,w_j\rangle
=
\delta_{ij}-\frac1N-\frac{2}{N}s_is_j.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\langle v_i,w_j\rangle
=
\begin{cases}
1-\frac3N,&i=j,\\
-\frac3N,&i\neq j,\ s_i=s_j,\\
\frac1N,&s_i\neq s_j.
\end{cases}
$$

For the unit-normalized simplex vertices, these values are multiplied by \(N/(N-1)\).

Thus \(V_N\) and \(W_N\) are each regular simplex ETFs, while the full compound usually has several cross-angles.  In particular, for \(N\geq6\) the compound should be treated as a two-simplex frame compound rather than as a single ETF unless a separate equiangular/projective condition is imposed and verified.

After normalizing all vertices to unit length, \(V_N\cup W_N\) is nevertheless a unit-norm tight frame.  Each component simplex has frame bound \(N/(N-1)\), and \(\rho_2\) is orthogonal, so the union has frame bound \(2N/(N-1)\).  Thus the negative result is only about equiangularity and Welch optimality, not tightness.

**Proof.**  Orthogonality of \(\rho_2\) gives the regular-simplex identities for \(W_N\).  Since \(v_i=e_i-\frac1N\mathbf 1\) and \(s\perp\mathbf 1\),

$$
\langle v_i,s\rangle=s_i.
$$

Therefore

$$
\langle v_i,w_j\rangle
=
\left\langle v_i,\ v_j-\frac{2s_j}{N}s\right\rangle
=
\delta_{ij}-\frac1N-\frac{2}{N}s_is_j.
$$

The three cases follow immediately. \(\square\)

## 4. The \(N=4\) Stella Octangula

For \(N=4\),

$$
s=(1,-1,1,-1)^T.
$$

The parity reflection sends

$$
w_i=\rho_2v_i=-v_{i+2}.
$$

Thus

$$
W_4=-C_4^2V_4.
$$

As an unordered set, \(W_4=-V_4\).  The original tetrahedron and the parity-dual tetrahedron are the two complementary tetrahedra of the stella octangula.

This equality is special to \(N=4\).  It happens because each parity class has exactly two vertices.  For even \(N>4\), the parity-dual simplex is still regular and canonical, but it is not merely the central inverse of the original simplex.

### Theorem 4.1: The Stella-Octangula Boundary Case

For \(N=4\),

$$
X_4=V_4\cup W_4=V_4\cup(-V_4).
$$

After unit normalization, \(X_4\) is the vertex set of a cube in \(H_4\cong\mathbb R^3\).  Equivalently, it is the compound of two complementary tetrahedra known as the stella octangula.  Its unordered off-diagonal Gram profile is

$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{inner product} & \text{multiplicity}\\
\hline
-3/4 & 4\\
-1/4 & 12\\
1/4 & 12.
\end{array}
$$

After unit normalization these values become \(-1,-1/3,1/3\).  Thus \(X_4\) is centrally symmetric and has four antipodal pairs.  Its coordinate stabilizer is \(S_4\), of order \(24\), while its full orthogonal stabilizer has order \(48\), the full symmetry group of the cube.  The polar polytope is the three-dimensional cross-polytope.

**Proof.**  The identity \(w_i=-v_{i+2}\) gives \(X_4=V_4\cup(-V_4)\), hence central symmetry and four antipodal pairs.  The Gram profile follows from the regular tetrahedron identities \(\langle v_i,v_i\rangle=3/4\) and \(\langle v_i,v_j\rangle=-1/4\) for \(i\neq j\), together with their negatives.  The coordinate action on the tetrahedron is the full label-permutation group \(S_4\).  Adding central inversion doubles the tetrahedral symmetry group to the full cube symmetry group of order \(48\).  The cube/cross-polytope polarity is the standard three-dimensional polarity between the cube and the octahedron. \(\square\)

### 4.2 Two Orientation-Reversing Involutions

There are two nearby but distinct orientation-reversing involutions:

1. **Galois conjugation:** \(\sigma_{-1}(v_i)=v_{-i}\).  This is a vertex permutation.  It preserves the original simplex as a set and reflects each cyclotomic rotation plane.
2. **Parity reflection:** \(\rho_2=I-2P_2\).  This is not generally a vertex permutation.  It produces the parity-dual simplex.

For \(N=4\), both are orientation-reversing and both touch the stella-octangula polarity, but they do different jobs.  Galois conjugation flips the \(\Phi_4\) plane; parity reflection flips the \(\Phi_2\) axis.  Their interaction is what makes the tetrahedral case unusually compact.

Indeed,

$$
-I_{H_4}=C_4^2\rho_2.
$$

So the central dual tetrahedron can be reached by parity reflection followed by a cyclic half-turn.

This is the promised Janus-polarity interpretation in intrinsic language.  The half-turn \(C_4^2\) preserves the orientation of the three-dimensional space \(H_4\), while \(\rho_2\) has determinant \(-1\).  Thus the operation that separates the two complementary tetrahedra is exactly the operation that reverses the \(H_4\) volume form.  In the quadray/rotor picture, choosing one tetrahedron rather than the other is therefore the same sign choice that fixes the handedness convention for the \(H_4\) cross-product formulas.  The identity above says that the ordinary antipodal polarity of the tetrahedron differs from this parity orientation flip only by the cyclic half-turn.

The Boolean Janus theorem in Section 6.6 reinterprets this single \(H_4\) sign choice as the first nontrivial coset in a binary simplex-code quotient.  Thus the stella-octangula bit is not an isolated \(N=4\) accident; it is the rank-one visible edge of a Hamming-code orientation hierarchy.

### 4.3 Quadray and Edge-Frame Reading

For \(N=4\), the centered simplex vectors \(v_0,v_1,v_2,v_3\) are the zero-sum version of quadray coordinates: a quadray coefficient vector \(q=(q_0,q_1,q_2,q_3)\) represents

$$
x=\sum_i q_i v_i,
$$

with the usual gauge freedom \(q\sim q+\lambda(1,1,1,1)\).  Since

$$
s=v_0-v_1+v_2-v_3,
$$

the parity reflection acts on quadray coefficients by

$$
q_i\longmapsto
q_i-\frac12\left(\sum_k(-1)^kq_k\right)(-1)^i,
$$

again modulo the all-ones gauge.  In particular,

$$
[1,0,0,0]\longmapsto
\left[\frac12,\frac12,-\frac12,\frac12\right]
\sim[0,0,-1,0],
$$

which is the coefficient form of \(\rho_2v_0=-v_2\).  Cyclically, \(\rho_2v_i=-v_{i+2}\).  Thus the quadray edge-frame statement and the spectral statement are the same local move: flip the alternating parity component, then read the result modulo the quadray gauge.

## 5. Even/Odd Dichotomy

The parity-dual construction depends on the \(\Phi_2\) block.  Therefore:

- if \(N\) is even, \(H_N^{[2]}\) is one-dimensional and defines \(\rho_2\);
- if \(N\) is odd, \(H_N^{[2]}\) is absent and there is no canonical parity reflection of this kind.

This does not mean odd \(N\) has no polar simplex.  The central inverse

$$
-V_N=\{-v_i\}
$$

exists for every \(N\).  The point is narrower: odd \(N\) has no cyclic \(\Phi_2\)-distinguished parity-dual simplex.  The even/odd dichotomy is therefore not about existence of a second regular simplex in general; it is about existence of a canonical cyclic parity dual.

Nor does it mean odd \(N\) has no spectral reflections at all.  Other cyclotomic blocks \(H_N^{[d]}\) can be reflected or conjugated, but for odd \(N\) none gives the distinguished one-dimensional parity axis used here.  Those higher-block reflections lead to a different problem: spectral compounds generated by multi-dimensional cyclotomic summands rather than by the \(\Phi_2\) line.

## 6. Relation to the \(N=4\) Intuition

The original stella-octangula intuition was:

> \(N=4\) has two interpenetrating tetrahedra.  Is this the first case of a general parity-dual simplex phenomenon?

There are three separate claims here.

1. **Canonical parity involution.**  For even \(N\), the reflection \(\rho_2\) exists, is canonical, and produces a second regular simplex frame.  This is proved above.
2. **Stella-octangula structure.**  For \(N=4\), this second simplex is the antipodal/complementary tetrahedron.  This is special: for even \(N\geq6\), \(\rho_2(V_N)\neq -V_N\) up to relabeling.
3. **Higher compound structure.**  For even \(N\geq6\), the real question is the structure of the \(2N\)-point compound

$$
V_N\cup W_N.
$$

It has two \(C_N\)-orbits and a \(\mathbb Z/2\) symmetry exchanging them.  Its coordinate and Gram stabilizers are computed below; the broader question of whether the compound sits naturally inside a known Coxeter or weight-configuration family remains open.

The generalized dual is not the Galois conjugation image of the simplex.  It is the reflection of the simplex across the hyperplane perpendicular to the \(\Phi_2\) parity component.

In field language:

- Galois conjugation is the unit \(a=-1\) acting by vertex relabeling \(i\mapsto -i\);
- parity reflection is the spectral reflection of the \(\Phi_2\) component.

In \(N=4\), both are visible because

$$
H_4=H_4^{[2]}\oplus H_4^{[4]}.
$$

This is why the \(N=4\) polarity can be described from either the stella-octangula geometry or the Gaussian-plane orientation, but the two descriptions should not be identified map-for-map.

For \(N=6\), the first non-stella even case,

$$
\rho_2(v_0)
=
v_0-\frac13s
=
\left(\frac12,\frac16,-\frac12,\frac16,-\frac12,\frac16\right),
$$

which is neither \(v_k\) nor \(-v_k\) for any \(k\).  Thus \(V_6\cup W_6\) is a genuine compound of two regular simplex ETFs in \(H_6\), not the union of a simplex and its antipode.

The stabilizer theorem below gives coordinate-permutation symmetry group order \(72=2(3!)^2\) in this case, exactly the group preserving or swapping the even/odd coordinate classes.  The abstract Gram automorphism group also contains the simplex-swap induced by \(\rho_2\), giving order \(144\).  Central inversion is not a symmetry of this compound.  Thus the first \(N\geq6\) case has no hidden stella-like enhancement at the coordinate-permutation level.

### 6.1 Stabilizer Theorem for \(N\geq6\)

The \(N=6\) example is the first instance of a general stabilizer pattern.

Let \(N=2m\geq6\), and write

$$
X_N=V_N\cup W_N.
$$

Let \(E\) and \(O\) be the even and odd index classes.  Define \(G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_N)\) to be the group of ambient coordinate permutations preserving \(X_N\), define \(\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathrm{Gram}}(X_N)\) to be the permutation group of the \(2N\) vertices preserving all pairwise inner products, and define

$$
O(X_N)=\{Q\in O(H_N):QX_N=X_N\}
$$

to be the ambient orthogonal stabilizer of the compound.

### Theorem 6.1: Parity-Class Stabilizer

For \(N=2m\geq6\),

$$
G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_N)
\cong
(S_m\times S_m)\rtimes C_2,
\qquad
|G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_N)|=2(m!)^2.
$$

The two \(S_m\) factors independently permute the even and odd coordinate classes, and the \(C_2\) factor swaps the two parity classes.  Moreover,

$$
\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathrm{Gram}}(X_N)
\cong
C_2\times\bigl((S_m\times S_m)\rtimes C_2\bigr),
\qquad
|\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathrm{Gram}}(X_N)|=4(m!)^2,
$$

where the extra \(C_2\) is the global simplex swap \(v_i\leftrightarrow w_i\) induced by \(\rho_2\).

Finally, the natural action map

$$
O(X_N)\to\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathrm{Gram}}(X_N)
$$

is an isomorphism.  Therefore the ambient orthogonal stabilizer has the same order \(4(m!)^2\).

**Proof.**  For \(N\geq6\), the off-diagonal inner products in \(X_N\) are

$$
\begin{array}{ccl}
\langle v_i,v_j\rangle=\langle w_i,w_j\rangle&=&-\frac1N\quad(i\neq j),\\[4pt]
\langle v_i,w_i\rangle&=&\frac{N-3}{N},\\[4pt]
\langle v_i,w_j\rangle&=&-\frac3N\quad(i\neq j,\ s_i=s_j),\\[4pt]
\langle v_i,w_j\rangle&=&\frac1N\quad(s_i\neq s_j).
\end{array}
$$

These four signed values are distinct.  Hence the value \((N-3)/N\) identifies the \(N\) matched pairs

$$
P_i=\{v_i,w_i\}.
$$

Every Gram automorphism must permute these pairs.  It may a priori flip \(v_i\) and \(w_i\) inside each pair.  But the value \(-1/N\) occurs between \(v_i,v_j\) and between \(w_i,w_j\) for every \(i\neq j\).  If two pairs were flipped inconsistently, such an edge would be sent to a cross-edge, whose value is either \(-3/N\) or \(1/N\), never \(-1/N\).  Therefore the inside-pair flip is global: either no pair is flipped, or every pair is flipped.  This gives the extra global \(C_2\) generated by \(\rho_2\).

After removing that global flip, a Gram automorphism has the form

$$
v_i\mapsto v_{\pi(i)},\qquad
w_i\mapsto w_{\pi(i)}.
$$

The cross values distinguish the relation \(s_i=s_j\) from \(s_i\neq s_j\).  Thus \(\pi\) must preserve the parity equivalence relation, meaning that it either preserves \(E\) and \(O\) separately or swaps them.  Conversely, any such \(\pi\), with or without the global \(v_i\leftrightarrow w_i\) swap, preserves the displayed inner-product table.  This proves the Gram automorphism statement.

For coordinate permutations, note that every ambient coordinate permutation sends \(V_N\) to itself.  Also \(V_N\cap W_N=\varnothing\) for \(N\geq6\): in the explicit coordinate formula for \(w_i\), the coordinates of parity opposite to \(i\) equal \(1/N\), while the same-parity coordinates away from \(i\) equal \(-3/N\); a simplex vertex has only one coordinate \((N-1)/N\) and all others \(-1/N\).  Therefore a coordinate permutation preserving \(X_N\) must send \(W_N\) to \(W_N\).  The same unique matched-pair value then forces \(w_i\mapsto w_{\pi(i)}\), and the preceding parity argument gives exactly the parity-preserving or parity-swapping coordinate permutations.  These form \((S_m\times S_m)\rtimes C_2\).

It remains to compare Gram automorphisms with orthogonal symmetries.  Any element of \(O(X_N)\) permutes \(X_N\) and preserves inner products, so it gives an element of \(\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathrm{Gram}}(X_N)\).  This map is injective because \(X_N\) spans \(H_N\): already \(V_N\) spans \(H_N\).  Conversely, let \(\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathrm{Gram}}(X_N)\).  Define a linear map on formal spans by \(T_\alpha x=\alpha(x)\) for \(x\in X_N\).  If \(\sum c_xx=0\), then the squared norm of the image is

$$
\left\lVert\sum c_x\alpha(x)\right\rVert^2
=
\sum_{x,y}c_xc_y\langle\alpha(x),\alpha(y)\rangle
=
\sum_{x,y}c_xc_y\langle x,y\rangle
=0,
$$

so the image relation is also zero.  Thus \(T_\alpha\) is well defined on \(H_N\), and the same calculation shows that it preserves inner products.  Hence \(T_\alpha\in O(X_N)\), proving surjectivity. \(\square\)

### 6.2 Type-A Coxeter Interpretation

Inside the type-A Coxeter group

$$
W(A_{N-1})\cong S_N,
$$

the coordinate stabilizer has a standard description in the language of parabolic subgroups and Young subgroups [Bjorner-Brenti].  The pointwise stabilizer of the parity vector \(s\) is the Young subgroup

$$
S_E\times S_O\cong S_m\times S_m.
$$

Equivalently, after reordering the coordinates so the even coordinates come first, this is the standard parabolic subgroup of type

$$
A_{m-1}\times A_{m-1}.
$$

The full coordinate stabilizer of the compound is the setwise stabilizer of the unordered bipartition \(\{E,O\}\), hence the normalizer of this equal-block Young subgroup:

$$
N_{S_N}(S_E\times S_O)
\cong
S_m\wr S_2
\cong
(S_m\times S_m)\rtimes C_2.
$$

It is also the centralizer in \(S_N\) of the rank-one projector \(P_2=\frac1Nss^T\), or equivalently of the parity reflection \(\rho_2=I-2P_2\), because a coordinate permutation commutes with \(P_2\) exactly when it sends \(s\) to \(\pm s\).  This should not be confused with the centralizer of a fixed-point-free involution in \(S_{2m}\), whose order is \(2^m m!\); the parity-dual stabilizer is the equal-block Young normalizer, of order \(2(m!)^2\).

### 6.3 Symmetry Summary

The symmetry layers are:

| Symmetry layer | Definition | \(N=4\) | \(N=2m\geq6\) |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Coordinate stabilizer | \(G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_N)\subset S_N\), coordinate permutations preserving \(X_N\) | \(S_4\), order \(24\) | \(S_m\wr S_2\), order \(2(m!)^2\) |
| Gram/orthogonal stabilizer | \(\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathrm{Gram}}(X_N)\cong O(X_N)\) | cube symmetry, order \(48\) | \(C_2\times(S_m\wr S_2)\), order \(4(m!)^2\) |
| Central inversion | whether \(-I_{H_N}\) preserves \(X_N\) | yes; it swaps the two tetrahedra | no |

Thus central inversion is the extra \(N=4\) stella-octangula symmetry, not a feature of the higher even-\(N\) parity-dual compounds.

### 6.4 Polyhedral Geometry of \(X_N\)

The convex hull has a simple product/free-sum description in the standard polytope sense [Ziegler].  Let

$$
a=\frac1N s,
\qquad
p_i=v_i-\frac{s_i}{N}s.
$$

Then

$$
v_i=p_i+s_i a,\qquad
w_i=p_i-s_i a,
$$

so as an unordered set

$$
X_N=\{p_i+a,\ p_i-a:\ 0\leq i<N\}.
$$

Let

$$
H_E=\{x:\operatorname{supp}(x)\subset E,\ \sum_{i\in E}x_i=0\},
\qquad
H_O=\{x:\operatorname{supp}(x)\subset O,\ \sum_{i\in O}x_i=0\}.
$$

For \(i\in E\), the \(p_i\) form a regular \((m-1)\)-simplex \(\Delta_E\subset H_E\).  For \(i\in O\), they form a regular \((m-1)\)-simplex \(\Delta_O\subset H_O\).  Moreover,

$$
H_N=H_E\oplus H_O\oplus\mathbb Rs
$$

orthogonally.

### Theorem 6.2: Convex-Hull Decomposition

For \(N=2m\),

$$
\operatorname{conv}(X_N)
=
\operatorname{conv}(\Delta_E\cup\Delta_O)+[-a,a].
$$

Equivalently, \(\operatorname{conv}(X_N)\) is the orthogonal prism over the free sum of two \((m-1)\)-simplices:

$$
\operatorname{conv}(X_N)
\cong
(\Delta_{m-1}\oplus\Delta_{m-1})\times I.
$$

For \(N=4\), this is the square prism, i.e. the cube.  For \(N=6\), it is a 5-polytope with 12 vertices: a prism over the free sum of two triangles.  Its polar is the bipyramid over \(\Delta_{m-1}\times\Delta_{m-1}\).

**Proof.**  The displayed identities give \(X_N=(Y+a)\cup(Y-a)\), where \(Y=\{p_i\}\).  Therefore

$$
\operatorname{conv}(X_N)=\operatorname{conv}(Y)+[-a,a].
$$

The even-index projections \(p_i\) have support only on \(E\), sum to zero on \(E\), and form a regular simplex in \(H_E\); the odd-index projections do the same in \(H_O\).  Since \(H_E\), \(H_O\), and \(\mathbb Rs\) are mutually orthogonal, \(\operatorname{conv}(Y)=\operatorname{conv}(\Delta_E\cup\Delta_O)\) is the free sum \(\Delta_E\oplus\Delta_O\), and adding the segment \([-a,a]\) gives an orthogonal prism.  The polar statement follows from the standard duality between free sums and products, together with the fact that the polar of a prism over \(B\) is the bipyramid over \(B^\circ\). \(\square\)

Thus the higher even-\(N\) object is not an unnamed shadow of the stella octangula.  It is a concrete vertex-transitive prism over a free sum of two equal simplices, with the \(N=4\) cube as the first and most symmetric boundary case.

### Corollary 6.5: Compound Gram Profile

For \(N=2m\geq6\), the unordered off-diagonal Gram profile of \(X_N\) is

$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{inner product} & \text{multiplicity}\\
\hline
-1/N & N(N-1)\\
(N-3)/N & N\\
-3/N & N(N-2)/2\\
1/N & N^2/2.
\end{array}
$$

After unit normalization, the absolute values are

$$
\frac1{N-1},\qquad \frac3{N-1},\qquad \frac{N-3}{N-1}.
$$

Thus the full compound is not a single ETF for \(N\geq6\).  For \(N=6\), the last two absolute values coincide; for \(N\geq8\), the coherence is \((N-3)/(N-1)\).

### Theorem 6.6: Boolean Janus Polarity

Let \(R=A/I\) be a finite quotient of a Dedekind domain with

$$
R\cong\mathbb F_2^k,
$$

equivalently \(I\) is a squarefree product of \(k\) distinct norm-two prime
ideals.  Form the regular simplex

$$
V_R=\{e_a-|R|^{-1}{\bf 1}:a\in R\}
\subset
H_R=\left\{x\in\mathbb R^R:\sum_{a\in R}x_a=0\right\}.
$$

Let \(R^\vee=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb F_2}(R,\mathbb F_2)\).  The
nonconstant binary character lines are indexed by \(R^\vee\setminus\{0\}\),
hence by the \(2^k-1\) nonzero linear functionals on \(R\).  Let \(B_R\) be
the elementary abelian block-sign group that changes signs independently on
these lines, and let

$$
K_R=\{T\in B_R:T(V_R)=V_R\}
$$

be the subgroup of block signs that only relabel the original simplex.  Then

$$
B_R\cong\mathbb F_2^{\,2^k-1},
\qquad
|B_R|=2^{2^k-1},
$$

and, under the identification of \(B_R\) with binary functions on
\(R^\vee\setminus\{0\}\),

$$
K_R
=
\{m\mapsto m(b):b\in R\}
\subset
\mathbb F_2^{\,2^k-1}.
$$

Thus \(K_R\) is the binary simplex code

$$
[\,2^k-1,\ k,\ 2^{k-1}\,],
$$

and the Boolean Janus orientation space is

$$
B_R/K_R,
\qquad
\dim_{\mathbb F_2}(B_R/K_R)=2^k-1-k.
$$

Equivalently, the distinct block-sign simplex images are indexed by the cosets
of the simplex code, the dual side of the Hamming-code structure of length
\(2^k-1\).  Since the simplex code is dual to the binary Hamming code
\([2^k-1,2^k-1-k,3]\), the quotient has the Hamming-code dimension.  In
particular:

1. \(k=1\) gives no orientation bit; \(R=\mathbb F_2\) is the degenerate line case.
2. \(k=2\) gives one bit.  This is the \(H_4\) Janus polarity/stella-octangula case, and it is also realized arithmetically by \(\mathcal O_{\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-7})}/(2)\).
3. \(k=3\) gives four bits and \(16\) distinct simplex images, as in the conductor-\(31\) cubic realization.

Thus Janus polarity lifts from a single \(H_4\) orientation bit to a
Hamming-code orientation hierarchy on completely split \((2)\) Dedekind
quotients.

**Proof.**  For \(R\cong\mathbb F_2^k\), every nontrivial additive character
has the form

$$
\chi_m(a)=(-1)^{m(a)},
\qquad
m\in R^\vee\setminus\{0\}.
$$

Each such character spans a real one-dimensional conductor line, so there is
one independent sign choice for each nonzero \(m\).  This gives
\(B_R\cong\mathbb F_2^{2^k-1}\).

Translations relabel the simplex.  Translation by \(b\in R\) acts on the
\(\chi_m\)-line by the sign \(\chi_m(b)=(-1)^{m(b)}\), so the translation
sign vector is exactly the binary word \(m\mapsto m(b)\) on
\(R^\vee\setminus\{0\}\).  Conversely, if a block sign \(T\) relabels
\(V_R\), then \(T\) commutes with translations and \(T(e_0-|R|^{-1}{\bf 1})\)
is some \(e_b-|R|^{-1}{\bf 1}\); hence \(T\) is translation by \(b\).  Thus
\(K_R\) is precisely the displayed translation-sign code.

The map \(b\mapsto(m\mapsto m(b))\) is injective, so the code has dimension
\(k\).  If \(b\neq0\), exactly half of the \(2^k\) linear functionals satisfy
\(m(b)=1\), and the zero functional is not among them.  Hence every nonzero
codeword has weight \(2^{k-1}\), proving that \(K_R\) is the binary simplex
code \([2^k-1,k,2^{k-1}]\).  The quotient dimension and the three
specializations follow immediately. \(\square\)

### 6.7 Small-\(N\) Consequences

The parity theorems above determine the parity-only compound for every even \(N\).  The small cases illustrate the transition:

1. \(N=4\) is exceptional: \(W_4=-V_4\) up to relabeling, central inversion preserves the compound, the coordinate stabilizer has order \(24\), and the full Gram automorphism group has order \(48\).
2. For \(N=2m\geq6\), central inversion does not preserve \(V_N\cup W_N\).  The coordinate stabilizer is the parity-class wreath product

$$
(S_m\times S_m)\rtimes C_2,
\qquad
|G_{\mathrm{coord}}|=2(m!)^2.
$$

3. The full Gram automorphism group also includes the \(\rho_2\)-swap between \(V_N\) and \(W_N\), so its order is \(4(m!)^2\) for \(N\geq6\).
4. The full compound is not a single ETF in the tested cases.  Each component simplex is an ETF, but the union has multiple normalized absolute inner products and does not meet the Welch bound for \(2N\) vectors in dimension \(N-1\).

The computational checks summarized in Appendix A verify these identities for the first few cases and compute the corresponding f-vectors from the exact prism/free-sum decomposition.

## 7. Boundary Between Theorems and Computation

Sections 1--6 give a closed parity-only theory, with Theorem 6.6 recording the Boolean Dedekind lift of the \(N=4\) orientation bit.  Section 8 studies the larger spectral compound obtained by changing signs on all cyclotomic blocks.  The present draft keeps these layers in one paper because the spectral construction explains why the \(\Phi_2\) reflection is the special one-dimensional boundary case.  The boundary is nevertheless real: the parity compound is a polytope/Coxeter object first, while the full spectral compound is a frame-theoretic and algebraic-combinatorial object first.  Theorems in Section 8 are stated only where an algebraic proof is given.  Tables not covered by those theorems are explicitly labeled as computational evidence or conjectural finite cases.

Two stabilizer groups must be kept separate.  The **coordinate stabilizer**

$$
G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X)\leq S_N
$$

consists of coordinate permutations preserving the vertex set.  The **full orthogonal stabilizer**

$$
O(X)=\{U\in O(H_N):U(X)=X\}
$$

may contain non-coordinate block-sign symmetries, and for mixed composites it is not classified here.  The spectral-affine subgroup displayed in Section 8.6 is a lower bound for \(O(X_N^{\mathrm{spec}})\), not a claim of equality.

The supporting computations are reproducible checks of exact identities, exact coordinate-pair color partitions, and finite face-lattice enumerations.  Their role is to sharpen conjectures and to prevent overstatement, not to replace the structural theorems.

## 8. Full Cyclotomic Reflection Compounds

The parity-dual simplex uses only one summand of the cyclotomic decomposition, namely the \(\Phi_2\) line.  The full decomposition suggests a broader reflection problem.

For every divisor \(d\mid N\), \(d>1\), let \(P_d\) be the orthogonal projector onto

$$
H_N^{[d]}=\ker\Phi_d(C_N),
$$

and define the block-sign involution

$$
\rho_d=I-2P_d.
$$

Because the cyclotomic summands are mutually orthogonal, these block-sign involutions commute.  Thus the first spectral reflection group is the elementary abelian group

$$
B_N=\langle \rho_d:\ d\mid N,\ d>1\rangle
\cong
(C_2)^{\tau(N)-1},
$$

where \(\tau(N)\) is the number of positive divisors of \(N\).  The parity-dual construction is the single-generator subcase \(d=2\).

There is already a useful exact structure theorem for this group action.  Let \(c_d(k)\) denote the Ramanujan sum

$$
c_d(k)=\sum_{a\in(\mathbb Z/d\mathbb Z)^\times}e^{2\pi iak/d}.
$$

The orthogonal projector onto \(H_N^{[d]}\) has entries

$$
(P_d)_{ij}=\frac1N c_d(i-j).
$$

Therefore if \(\epsilon=(\epsilon_d)_{d\mid N,\ d>1}\), \(\epsilon_d\in\{\pm1\}\), and

$$
T_\epsilon=\prod_{\epsilon_d=-1}\rho_d,
$$

then

$$
\langle v_i,T_\epsilon v_j\rangle
=
\frac1N\sum_{\substack{d\mid N\\d>1}}\epsilon_d\,c_d(i-j).
$$

This is the unnormalized centered-simplex formula.  For unit-normalized
vertices, the same cross-Gram entries are multiplied by \(N/(N-1)\), so the
Ramanujan denominator in the normalized angle set below is \(N-1\), not \(N\).
This gives a closed arithmetic formula for every cross-Gram matrix in the full
block-sign orbit without making any Grassmannian-optimality claim.

### Proposition 8.1: Block-Sign Orbit Size

Let \(b=\tau(N)-1\).  The number of distinct simplex sets in the orbit

$$
\{T(V_N):T\in B_N\}
$$

is

$$
\begin{cases}
2^b,&N\ \text{odd},\\
2^{b-1},&N\ \text{even}.
\end{cases}
$$

For even \(N\), the unique nontrivial block-sign element fixing \(V_N\) as a set is the cyclic half-turn \(C_N^{N/2}\).  In block signs, it is given by

$$
\epsilon_d=
\begin{cases}
-1,&N/d\ \text{odd},\\
+1,&N/d\ \text{even}.
\end{cases}
$$

Distinct simplex sets in this orbit are vertex-disjoint.

**Proof.**  Suppose \(T\in B_N\) and \(T(V_N)=V_N\).  Every \(T\in B_N\) commutes with \(C_N\), so if \(T v_0=v_b\), then

$$
T v_i=T C_N^i v_0=C_N^iT v_0=v_{i+b}.
$$

Thus \(T\) acts on \(V_N\) as a cyclic shift.  But every element of \(B_N\) is a real symmetric operator, being a signed sum of orthogonal projectors.  The only symmetric cyclic shifts are \(I\), and, when \(N\) is even, \(C_N^{N/2}\).  The action of \(C_N^{N/2}\) on the \(d\)-th cyclotomic block is multiplication by \((-1)^k\) for frequencies \(k=(N/d)a\), \(a\in(\mathbb Z/d\mathbb Z)^\times\).  This sign is constant on the block and equals \(-1\) exactly when \(N/d\) is odd.  Hence \(B_N\cap\operatorname{Stab}(V_N)\) is trivial for odd \(N\) and has order two for even \(N\), giving the stated orbit sizes.

Finally, if two image simplices \(T(V_N)\) and \(S(V_N)\) share one vertex, then \(S^{-1}T\) sends some \(v_i\) to some \(v_j\).  Since \(S^{-1}T\) commutes with \(C_N\), it sends every \(v_{i+k}\) to \(v_{j+k}\), hence maps \(V_N\) to itself.  Therefore \(T(V_N)=S(V_N)\).  Distinct image simplices are vertex-disjoint. \(\square\)

Define the full block-sign spectral compound

$$
X_N^{\mathrm{spec}}
=
\bigcup_{T\in B_N}T(V_N),
$$

with duplicate simplex sets removed.  The proposition shows that \(X_N^{\mathrm{spec}}\) contains \(N2^b\) vertices for odd \(N\) and \(N2^{b-1}\) vertices for even \(N\), except for the degenerate \(N=2\) line case.  Since the all-block sign is \(-I_{H_N}\), the full spectral compound is centrally symmetric and has ordinary coherence \(1\); the meaningful packing statistic is its non-antipodal, projective coherence.

### Theorem 8.2: Ramanujan Few-Angle Tight Frame

Let \(N\geq3\), let

$$
M_N=
\begin{cases}
2^{\tau(N)-1},&N\ \text{odd},\\
2^{\tau(N)-2},&N\ \text{even},
\end{cases}
$$

be the number of distinct simplex images in Proposition 8.1, and let

$$
\widehat X_N^{\mathrm{spec}}
=
\left\{\sqrt{\frac{N}{N-1}}\,x:\ x\in X_N^{\mathrm{spec}}\right\}
$$

be the unit-normalized spectral compound.  Then \(\widehat X_N^{\mathrm{spec}}\) is a unit-norm tight frame of \(NM_N\) vectors for \(H_N\), with frame bound

$$
A_N=\frac{NM_N}{N-1}.
$$

Equivalently, its frame operator is

$$
\sum_{\hat x\in\widehat X_N^{\mathrm{spec}}}\hat x\hat x^T
=
\frac{NM_N}{N-1}I_{H_N}.
$$

Moreover, its normalized inner products are governed by Ramanujan sums.  If \(K_N=B_N\cap\operatorname{Stab}(V_N)\) and \(\Gamma_N=B_N/K_N\), then the realized projective absolute inner products are exactly

$$
\mathcal A_N^{\mathrm{proj}}
=
\left\{
\left|
\frac{1}{N-1}
\sum_{\substack{d\mid N\\d>1}}\epsilon_d c_d(k)
\right|:
[\epsilon]\in\Gamma_N,\ k\in\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z,\ 
\left|
\frac{1}{N-1}
\sum_{\substack{d\mid N\\d>1}}\epsilon_d c_d(k)
\right|<1
\right\}.
$$

Here any representative \(\epsilon\) of the coset \([\epsilon]\) may be used; in the even case, multiplying by the half-turn kernel element only relabels \(k\).

Thus \(X_N^{\mathrm{spec}}\) is an exact arithmetic few-angle tight frame.  Its ordinary coherence is \(1\), because the all-block sign element is \(-I_{H_N}\) and hence the compound contains antipodal pairs.  The useful packing statistic is therefore

$$
\mu_N^{\mathrm{proj}}
=
\max \mathcal A_N^{\mathrm{proj}},
$$

the largest non-antipodal projective absolute inner product.

**Proof.**  A unit-normalized regular simplex in \(H_N\) has \(N\) unit vectors in dimension \(N-1\), hence frame bound \(N/(N-1)\).  Each \(T\in B_N\) is orthogonal on \(H_N\), so every image \(T(V_N)\), after unit normalization, has the same frame operator

$$
\frac{N}{N-1}I_{H_N}.
$$

After duplicate simplex sets are removed, there are \(M_N\) such images.  Summing the \(M_N\) identical frame operators gives

$$
\frac{NM_N}{N-1}I_{H_N}.
$$

For the angle formula, take two normalized vertices coming from image simplices \(T_\alpha(V_N)\) and \(T_\beta(V_N)\).  Since the block-sign group is abelian and each element is an involution,

$$
\left\langle
\sqrt{\frac{N}{N-1}}\,T_\alpha v_i,\
\sqrt{\frac{N}{N-1}}\,T_\beta v_j
\right\rangle
=
\frac{N}{N-1}\langle v_i,T_{\alpha\beta}v_j\rangle.
$$

Applying the Ramanujan cross-Gram formula gives

$$
\frac{1}{N-1}
\sum_{\substack{d\mid N\\d>1}}\alpha_d\beta_d\,c_d(i-j).
$$

Passing to cosets in \(\Gamma_N=B_N/K_N\) removes duplicate simplex images.  In the even case, changing a representative by the half-turn element only cyclically relabels the vertex index, so the displayed set of values is independent of the representative choice.  Finally, deleting the absolute value \(1\) removes identical and antipodal pairs, leaving exactly the projective non-antipodal angle set. \(\square\)

This turns the full block-sign compound into a positive frame-theoretic object rather than merely a failed ETF: it is a canonical unit-norm tight frame with an explicit arithmetic projective angle set.  More concretely, each \(N\times N\) cross-Gram block between two image simplices is circulant, because its entries depend only on \(i-j\).  Therefore the full Gram matrix is block-circulant with circulant vertex blocks.  The spectral symmetries contain

$$
\frac{B_N\times C_N}{K_N}
\leq
O(P_N^{\mathrm{spec}}),
$$

and, since unit relabelings \(i\mapsto ai\) preserve every Ramanujan sum \(c_d(i-j)\), in fact contain the larger spectral-affine subgroup

$$
\frac{B_N\times\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)}{K_N}
\leq
O(P_N^{\mathrm{spec}}).
$$

This is only a lower bound for the full orthogonal stabilizer, but it is an automatic consequence of the Ramanujan formula, not a separate computation.

The same formula also identifies the entries as cyclotomic traces:

$$
c_d(k)=\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathbb Q(\zeta_d)/\mathbb Q}(\zeta_d^k).
$$

Thus the construction sits next to harmonic and finite Gabor frame constructions, and also near the cyclotomic trace language that appears around SIC-POVM and Zauner-type problems [Strohmer-Heath; Zauner; Renes-Blume-Kohout-Scott-Caves].  No SIC or Gabor optimality claim is made here; the point is only that the Gram matrices are built from the same primitive root-of-unity sums rather than from an ad hoc angle table.

### 8.2.1 Projective Packing Baseline

Because \(X_N^{\mathrm{spec}}\) is centrally symmetric, the ordinary coherence is always \(1\).  The packing comparison should therefore be made in real projective space after identifying antipodal pairs.  Put

$$
d_N=N-1,\qquad
L_N=\frac{|X_N^{\mathrm{spec}}|}{2}.
$$

The projective Welch bound is

$$
\mu_{\mathrm W}(L_N,d_N)
=
\sqrt{\frac{L_N-d_N}{d_N(L_N-1)}}.
$$

This is the usual ETF/Grassmannian-frame baseline [Welch; Delsarte-Goethals-Seidel; Strohmer-Heath].

When

$$
\binom{d_N+1}{2}<L_N\leq\binom{d_N+2}{3},
$$

the second real projective Levenshtein bound applies:

$$
\mu_{\mathrm L,2}(L_N,d_N)
=
\sqrt{
\frac{3L_N-d_N(d_N+2)}
{(d_N+2)(L_N-d_N)}
}.
$$

This is the first Levenshtein refinement relevant to real projective line systems [Levenshtein].

The small spectral compounds compare as follows.  The "excess over bound" column is \(\mu_N^{\mathrm{proj}}\) minus the active lower bound, using the Levenshtein bound only when it improves on Welch in the displayed range.  It is the amount by which the construction misses the relevant packing lower bound.

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c|c|c}
N&d_N&|X_N^{\mathrm{spec}}|&L_N&\mu_N^{\mathrm{proj}}&\text{bound}&\text{excess over bound}\\
\hline
5&4&10&5&1/4&1/4&0.000000\\
6&5&24&12&3/5&\sqrt{7/55}&0.243247\\
7&6&14&7&1/6&1/6&0.000000\\
8&7&32&16&5/7&\sqrt{3/35}&0.421516\\
9&8&36&18&5/8&\sqrt{5/68}&0.353837\\
10&9&40&20&7/9&\sqrt{11/171}&0.524149\\
12&11&192&96&9/11&\sqrt{29/221}&0.455936
\end{array}
$$

The prime rows \(N=5,7\) are exactly the antipodal simplex and saturate Welch, as Theorem 8.3 predicts.  The composite rows do not approach the Welch or Levenshtein benchmarks.  This is useful negative information: the spectral compound is tight and arithmetically rigid, but its small composite instances are not Grassmannian packings.  Their value is the exact Ramanujan few-angle structure, the block-circulant symmetry, and the divisor-scheme stabilizer theory developed below.

### 8.3 Convex Hulls: Polar Form and the Prime Family

Let

$$
P_N^{\mathrm{spec}}=\operatorname{conv}(X_N^{\mathrm{spec}})
$$

and let

$$
Q_N=[-1,1]^N\cap H_N.
$$

Choose one block-sign representative \(T\) for each distinct simplex image in \(B_N/K_N\).  Since each \(T\in B_N\) is an orthogonal self-adjoint involution on \(H_N\),

$$
\langle y,Tv_i\rangle
=
\langle Ty,v_i\rangle
=
(Ty)_i
$$

for every \(y\in H_N\).  Therefore the polar of the full spectral hull is

$$
\left(P_N^{\mathrm{spec}}\right)^\circ
=
\bigcap_{T\in B_N/K_N}TQ_N.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
y\in\left(P_N^{\mathrm{spec}}\right)^\circ
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
|(Ty)_i|\leq 1
\quad\text{for every representative }T\text{ and every }i.
$$

Thus the full block-sign polytope is dual to an exact intersection of block-signed central cube slices.  This is the safe all-\(N\) convex-hull description.  It specializes cleanly in the prime case.

### Theorem 8.3: Prime Spectral Hull

Let \(p\geq3\) be prime.  Then

$$
X_p^{\mathrm{spec}}=V_p\cup(-V_p),
$$

and

$$
P_p^{\mathrm{spec}}
=
\operatorname{conv}(V_p\cup -V_p)
=
Q_p^\circ,
\qquad
Q_p=[-1,1]^p\cap H_p.
$$

The coordinate stabilizer is \(S_p\), and the full orthogonal stabilizer is

$$
C_2\times S_p,
$$

where \(C_2\) is generated by central inversion.

Writing \(p=2r+1\), the polytope is simplicial.  Its facets are indexed by sign vectors in \(\{-1,0,1\}^p\) with \(r\) entries \(+1\), \(r\) entries \(-1\), and one zero entry.  In particular the number of facets is

$$
p\binom{p-1}{(p-1)/2}.
$$

More generally, for \(0\leq k\leq p-2\), its \(k\)-face number is

$$
f_k
=
\sum_{\substack{a+b=k+1\\ |a-b|<p-k-1}}
\frac{p!}{a!\,b!\,(p-a-b)!}.
$$

**Proof.**  For prime \(p\), the only nontrivial cyclotomic block in \(H_p\) is \(H_p^{[p]}=H_p\).  Hence the nontrivial block-sign operator is \(-I_{H_p}\), so \(X_p^{\mathrm{spec}}=V_p\cup(-V_p)\).  The polar identity follows from the general formula above with only one cube-slice condition:

$$
\left(\operatorname{conv}(V_p\cup -V_p)\right)^\circ
=
\{y\in H_p: |\langle y,v_i\rangle|\leq1\ \forall i\}
=
[-1,1]^p\cap H_p,
$$

because \(\langle y,v_i\rangle=y_i\) for \(y\in H_p\).

An orthogonal symmetry of \(V_p\cup(-V_p)\) preserves antipodal pairs, and the induced action on the \(p\) pairs is exactly the regular-simplex symmetry group \(S_p\).  Central inversion supplies the independent \(C_2\), proving the stabilizer statement.

It remains to count faces.  A face is exposed by a vector \(y\in Q_p\).  If the exposed vertices are \(v_i\) for \(i\in A\) and \(-v_j\) for \(j\in B\), then \(y_i=1\) on \(A\), \(y_j=-1\) on \(B\), and the remaining \(p-|A|-|B|\) coordinates must lie strictly between \(-1\) and \(1\) while summing to \(|B|-|A|\).  This is possible exactly when

$$
\bigl||A|-|B|\bigr|<p-|A|-|B|.
$$

For a \(k\)-face one has \(|A|+|B|=k+1\), giving the displayed formula.  At \(k=p-2\), this forces \(|A|=|B|=r\), so the facets have one zero coordinate and \(2r=p-1\) vertices.  Hence the prime hull is simplicial. \(\square\)

### 8.4 Composite Hulls and Stabilizer Cautions

The first composite full spectral hull is already different from both the prime antipodal hull and the parity-only prism:

$$
N=6,\qquad |X_6^{\mathrm{spec}}|=24.
$$

Exact facet enumeration gives the f-vector

$$
(f_0,f_1,f_2,f_3,f_4)=(24,108,192,144,38),
$$

with facets having either \(8\) or \(12\) vertices.  This rules out a naive extension of the prime simplicial model.

### Theorem 8.4: The \(N=6\) Composite Spectral Hull

For \(N=6\), the full block-sign spectral hull is the orthogonal prism over the free sum of two centrally symmetric hexagons:

$$
P_6^{\mathrm{spec}}
\cong
(\mathsf H_E\oplus\mathsf H_O)\times I,
$$

where

$$
\mathsf H_E=\operatorname{conv}(\Delta_E\cup-\Delta_E),
\qquad
\mathsf H_O=\operatorname{conv}(\Delta_O\cup-\Delta_O)
$$

are regular hexagons in the even and odd zero-sum planes.  Consequently,

$$
f(P_6^{\mathrm{spec}})=(24,108,192,144,38),
$$

and its facets have either \(8\) or \(12\) vertices.

**Proof.**  Use the orthogonal decomposition

$$
H_6=H_E\oplus H_O\oplus\mathbb Rs,
$$

where \(H_E\) and \(H_O\) are the zero-sum subspaces supported on the even and odd coordinates, and \(s=(1,-1,1,-1,1,-1)^T\).  As in Theorem 6.2, write

$$
a=\frac16s,
\qquad
v_i=p_i+s_i a.
$$

For \(i\in E\), the vectors \(p_i\) form a regular triangle \(\Delta_E\subset H_E\); for \(i\in O\), they form a regular triangle \(\Delta_O\subset H_O\).

The block-sign group is generated by \(\rho_2,\rho_3,\rho_6\).  The reflection \(\rho_2\) flips only the parity axis \(\mathbb Rs\).  On \(H_E\oplus H_O\), the half-turn \(C_6^3\) identifies the even and odd planes.  The cyclotomic blocks \(H_6^{[3]}\) and \(H_6^{[6]}\) are respectively the \(+1\) and \(-1\) eigenspaces of this even/odd swap.  Therefore, after identifying the two planes by \(C_6^3\),

$$
\rho_6(x_E,x_O)=(x_O,x_E),
\qquad
\rho_3(x_E,x_O)=(-x_O,-x_E).
$$

Thus the \(\langle\rho_3,\rho_6\rangle\)-orbit of the projected simplex vertices in \(H_E\oplus H_O\) is exactly

$$
(\Delta_E\cup-\Delta_E)\cup(\Delta_O\cup-\Delta_O),
$$

with the two terms lying in orthogonal summands.  Its convex hull is

$$
\mathsf H_E\oplus\mathsf H_O.
$$

The independent action of \(\rho_2\) supplies both parity translates \(+a\) and \(-a\) over every projected vertex.  Hence the full hull is the cartesian product

$$
P_6^{\mathrm{spec}}
\cong
(\mathsf H_E\oplus\mathsf H_O)\times[-a,a].
$$

It remains to count faces.  A hexagon has \(f=(6,6)\).  For the free sum \(P=\mathsf H_E\oplus\mathsf H_O\), the proper faces are obtained by joining proper faces from the two factors, including the empty face.  Hence

$$
f(P)=(12,48,72,36).
$$

Explicitly, the \(12\) vertices come from the two hexagons, the \(48\) edges are the \(12\) internal hexagon edges together with \(6\cdot6\) cross edges, the \(72\) two-faces are edge-vertex joins, and the \(36\) facets are edge-edge joins.

For a prism over a \(4\)-polytope \(P\),

$$
f_k(P\times I)=2f_k(P)+f_{k-1}(P)
\quad(0\leq k\leq3),
$$

and the facets are the two caps plus prisms over the facets of \(P\).  Therefore

$$
f(P_6^{\mathrm{spec}})
=(24,108,192,144,38).
$$

The \(36\) side facets are prisms over edge-edge joins, hence have \(8\) vertices, while the two caps are copies of \(\mathsf H_E\oplus\mathsf H_O\), hence have \(12\) vertices. \(\square\)

For \(N=8,9,10\), finite facet searches give numerical facet candidates that are then reconstructed and checked in exact rational arithmetic before the face numbers are counted.  This gives the following seeded composite f-vectors:

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
N&\text{f-vector}&\text{facet sizes}\\
\hline
8&(32,192,496,684,528,216,38)&16\\
9&(36,486,2616,6660,8928,6378,2196,252)&12,16\\
10&(40,380,2020,6640,13660,17400,13200,5400,902)&16,20
\end{array}
$$

**Conjecture 8.4A: Small Composite Spectral Hulls.**  The displayed rows are the true f-vectors and facet-size profiles of \(P_N^{\mathrm{spec}}\) for \(N=8,9,10\).  A structural proof should derive these numbers from the cyclotomic divisor lattice, rather than from finite enumeration.

**Problem 8.4B: Cyclotomic Product/Free-Sum Structure.**  The \(N=6\) theorem should be read as the first solved case of a broader structural problem: determine when composite spectral hulls decompose as iterated prisms, free sums, products, or CRT products of lower cyclotomic polygonal pieces.  The answer cannot simply be "the \(N=6\) model repeats."  The case \(N=8\) already has a different profile: the nontrivial blocks are \(\Phi_2,\Phi_4,\Phi_8\), the \(\Phi_8\) block is four-dimensional over \(\mathbb R\), all computed facets have \(16\) vertices, and the coordinate stabilizer is the truncated binary-tree group \(S_4\wr S_2\) from Theorem 8.5 rather than a two-hexagon free sum.  Thus the structural conjecture is not a single product formula, but a divisor-lattice recipe explaining which cyclotomic pieces become prism directions, which become free-sum factors, and which remain coupled through p-adic or CRT fibers.

The \(N=12\) full spectral hull remains outside the current face-lattice computation.  Its role in the present paper is therefore diagnostic: it marks the point at which the known exact polar-intersection formula is not yet accompanied by a usable face-lattice theorem.

The corresponding coordinate-stabilizer table uses exact coordinate-pair color upper bounds, matched against explicit lower-bound groups:

8&1152&S_4\wr S_2\\
9&1296&S_3\wr S_3\\
10&28800&S_5\wr S_2\\
12&48&\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z).
\end{array}
$$

The rows \(N=8,9\) are explained by the prime-power theorem below.  The rows \(N=6,10,12\) are proved in Section 8.6 by the even semiprime and \(N=12\) rigidity theorems.

### Theorem 8.5: Prime-Power Coordinate Stabilizers

Let \(N=p^r\) be a prime power, and let \(G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_N^{\mathrm{spec}})\leq S_N\) be the group of ambient coordinate permutations preserving the full block-sign spectral compound.

If \(p\) is odd and \(r\geq2\), then \(G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_N^{\mathrm{spec}})\) is the automorphism group of the rooted \(p\)-adic congruence tree on \(\mathbb Z/p^r\mathbb Z\).  Equivalently, it is the group of permutations preserving every congruence relation

$$
i\equiv j\pmod {p^a},
\qquad
1\leq a\leq r-1.
$$

Thus

$$
G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_{p^r}^{\mathrm{spec}})
\cong
\underbrace{S_p\wr S_p\wr\cdots\wr S_p}_{r\ \mathrm{copies}},
\qquad
|G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_{p^r}^{\mathrm{spec}})|
=
\prod_{a=0}^{r-1}(p!)^{p^a}.
$$

For powers of two, \(N=2^r\), the case \(r=2\) is exceptional: \(X_4^{\mathrm{spec}}=V_4\cup(-V_4)\), so the coordinate stabilizer is \(S_4\).  If \(r\geq3\), the stabilizer preserves the congruence relations modulo

$$
2,4,\ldots,2^{r-2},
$$

and acts as the full symmetric group \(S_4\) inside each residue class modulo \(2^{r-2}\).  Hence

$$
|G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_{2^r}^{\mathrm{spec}})|
=
(4!)^{2^{r-2}}\prod_{a=0}^{r-3}2^{2^a}.
$$

For example, \(N=8\) gives \(S_4\wr S_2\), of order \(1152\), and \(N=9\) gives \(S_3\wr S_3\), of order \(1296\).

**Proof.**  Write the nontrivial divisors of \(p^r\) as \(p,p^2,\ldots,p^r\), and write a block sign as \(\epsilon=(\epsilon_1,\ldots,\epsilon_r)\).  For a vertex \(x=T_\epsilon v_i\), the \(j\)-th coordinate is

$$
x_j
=
\frac{1}{p^r}
\sum_{a=1}^r\epsilon_a c_{p^a}(j-i).
$$

The prime-power Ramanujan sums satisfy

$$
c_{p^a}(h)=
\begin{cases}
p^{a-1}(p-1),& v_p(h)\geq a,\\
-p^{a-1},& v_p(h)=a-1,\\
0,& v_p(h)<a-1,
\end{cases}
$$

with the first case applying for all \(a\) when \(h=0\).  Thus every block-sign vertex is a p-adic shell function around its center \(i\).

For odd \(p\), a permutation preserving all congruence relations modulo \(p^a\), \(1\leq a\leq r-1\), preserves the value \(v_p(j-i)\) for every pair of distinct coordinates.  The shell formula therefore sends \(T_\epsilon v_i\) to \(T_\epsilon v_{\pi(i)}\), so the rooted congruence-tree automorphism group is contained in the coordinate stabilizer.

Conversely, any coordinate permutation preserving \(X_N^{\mathrm{spec}}\) preserves the exact coordinate-pair color

$$
\mathcal C(i,j)
=
\{(x_i,x_j):x\in X_N^{\mathrm{spec}}\}.
$$

Substituting the displayed Ramanujan formula shows that, for odd \(p\),

$$
\mathcal C(i,j)=\mathcal C(i',j')
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
v_p(i-j)=v_p(i'-j').
$$

Hence every coordinate stabilizer preserves the full p-adic congruence tree, giving equality and the stated iterated-wreath order.

For \(p=2\), the same argument holds down to the congruence level \(2^{r-2}\).  The last two binary levels fuse because the half-turn kernel identifies the top block-sign distinction.  More concretely, inside a four-point residue class modulo \(2^{r-2}\), the last two signs \((\epsilon_{r-1},\epsilon_r)\) make the coordinate pattern either one distinguished value and three equal complementary values, or the complementary pattern.  A full \(S_4\) permutation of that four-point fiber merely changes the center and the last two block signs.  Therefore the lower binary congruence-tree automorphisms, together with these \(S_4\) fiber actions, preserve the vertex set.

The coordinate-pair color calculation gives the matching upper bound:

$$
\mathcal C(i,j)=\mathcal C(i',j')
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\min(v_2(i-j),r-2)=\min(v_2(i'-j'),r-2).
$$

Thus the stabilizer is exactly the stated truncated binary tree group with \(S_4\) fibers.  Counting independent symmetric-group choices at each tree node gives the displayed order. \(\square\)

### 8.6 Divisor Schemes and Mixed-Composite Evidence

The prime-power theorem leaves the mixed divisor-lattice cases.  The first mixed rigidity case and two infinite mixed families are proved below.  The \(N=6,10,12,15\) rows in the table are theorems; the other mixed rows are exact coordinate-pair color upper bounds matched against explicit generator lower bounds:

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
N&|G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_N^{\mathrm{spec}})|&\text{identified coordinate stabilizer}\\
\hline
6&72&S_3\wr S_2\\
10&28800&S_5\wr S_2\\
12&48&\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z)\\
15&720&S_3\times S_5\\
18&559872&S_3^6\rtimes\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z)\\
20&960&\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z)\times S_5.
\end{array}
$$

These cases show that mixed composites do not follow one uniform wreath-product rule.  They suggest that the correct invariant is not prime factorization alone, but the divisor-lattice profile visible to the Ramanujan coordinate shells.

For \(h\in\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z\), define the coordinate-pair color

$$
\mathcal C_N(h)
=
\{(x_0,x_h):x\in X_N^{\mathrm{spec}}\}.
$$

Translation invariance makes this a function of the difference \(h=j-i\).

The next theorem supplies the coordinate-level association-scheme bridge and
proves the first mixed-composite rigidity row.

**Theorem 8.6: Divisor-Scheme Fusion and \(N=12\) Rigidity.**  For each divisor \(g\mid N\), let

$$
R_g=\{(i,j)\in(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^2:\gcd(N,j-i)=g\}.
$$

The relations \(R_g\) form a symmetric Schurian association scheme \(\mathfrak D_N\) on \(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z\) [Bannai-Ito; Higman; Zieschang].  The coordinate-pair colors of \(X_N^{\mathrm{spec}}\) are a fusion of this divisor scheme: if \(h'\equiv uh\pmod N\) for some unit \(u\in(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^\times\), then

$$
\mathcal C_N(h')=\mathcal C_N(h).
$$

Consequently every coordinate stabilizer satisfies

$$
G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_N^{\mathrm{spec}})
\leq
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak F_N),
$$

where \(\mathfrak F_N\) is the color fusion of \(\mathfrak D_N\) induced by the sets \(\mathcal C_N(h)\).

For \(N=12\), the color fusion is the full nonzero divisor scheme and

$$
G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_{12}^{\mathrm{spec}})
=
\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z),
$$

of order \(48\).

**Proof.**  The affine group

$$
\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)
=
\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z\rtimes(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^\times
$$

acts transitively on \(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z\).  Its orbitals on ordered pairs are exactly the relations \(R_g\): translations reduce a pair to its difference, and the unit group acts transitively on all differences with the same value of \(\gcd(N,h)\).  Since \(-1\) is a unit, the relations are symmetric.  Thus \(\mathfrak D_N\) is the Schurian association scheme attached to this affine action.

For the fusion statement, write a block-sign vertex centered at \(a\) as

$$
x_j
=
\frac1N
\sum_{\substack{d\mid N\\d>1}}
\epsilon_d c_d(j-a).
$$

If \(u\) is a unit modulo \(N\), then it is a unit modulo every \(d\mid N\), and Ramanujan sums satisfy \(c_d(ut)=c_d(t)\).  Therefore the substitution \(a\mapsto ua\) identifies the pair-color set for \(h\) with the pair-color set for \(uh\).  Hence the spectral pair colors are constant on the unit orbits of differences, so they are a fusion of the divisor scheme.  Every coordinate permutation preserving the vertex set preserves the exact pair-color relation, giving the displayed upper bound.

It remains to identify the \(N=12\) fusion and its automorphism group.  Direct substitution of \(d=2,3,4,6,12\) in the displayed coordinate formula gives five distinct nonzero colors.  After clearing denominators by multiplying coordinates by \(12\), the following two elementary invariants already distinguish them:

$$
\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
g=\gcd(12,h)&1&2&3&4&6\\
\hline
|\mathcal C_{12}(h)|&54&46&38&30&38\\
\sum_{(a,b)\in 12\mathcal C_{12}(h)}ab&-142&-54&-74&-134&-218.
\end{array}
$$

Thus \(\mathfrak F_{12}=\mathfrak D_{12}\) on nonzero differences.  Under the Chinese-remainder identification

$$
\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z\cong\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z,
$$

the divisor scheme \(\mathfrak D_{12}\) is the direct product of the divisor schemes on \(\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z\) and \(\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z\).  The relation \(g=4\) has four connected components, the fibers of the projection to \(\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z\), and the relation \(g=3\) has three connected components, the fibers of the projection to \(\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z\).  Hence any automorphism of \(\mathfrak D_{12}\) splits over the two CRT coordinates.  The first factor has automorphism group \(\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z)\), of order \(8\), because its proper nontrivial relation is the matching \(\{0,2\},\{1,3\}\).  The second has automorphism group \(S_3=\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z)\), of order \(6\).  Therefore

$$
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak D_{12})
\cong
\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z)
\times
\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z)
\cong
\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z),
$$

of order \(48\).

The reverse inclusion comes from the spectral construction itself: translations are powers of \(C_{12}\), and unit multiplications preserve every cyclotomic projector \(P_d\), hence preserve the full block-sign orbit.  Thus

$$
\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z)
\leq
G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_{12}^{\mathrm{spec}})
\leq
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak D_{12})
=
\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z),
$$

which proves equality. \(\square\)

The next theorem proves the simplest infinite mixed family.  It explains the parity-collapse rows \(N=6\) and \(N=10\) in the table.

**Theorem 8.7: Even Semiprime Parity-Collapse Stabilizers.**  Let \(p\) be an odd prime and \(N=2p\).  Then the coordinate-pair colors of \(X_{2p}^{\mathrm{spec}}\) collapse exactly to the parity relation.  Consequently

$$
G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_{2p}^{\mathrm{spec}})
\cong
S_p\wr S_2,
$$

the setwise stabilizer of the even/odd coordinate bipartition, of order \(2(p!)^2\).

**Proof.**  The nontrivial divisors of \(N=2p\) are \(2,p,2p\).  For a block-sign vertex \(x=T_\epsilon v_a\), clear denominators and write

$$
N x_j
=
\epsilon_2c_2(j-a)
+\epsilon_pc_p(j-a)
+\epsilon_{2p}c_{2p}(j-a).
$$

The relevant Ramanujan values are

$$
c_2(t)=
\begin{cases}
1,&t\ \text{even},\\
-1,&t\ \text{odd},
\end{cases}
\qquad
c_p(t)=
\begin{cases}
p-1,&p\mid t,\\
-1,&p\nmid t,
\end{cases}
\qquad
c_{2p}(t)=c_2(t)c_p(t).
$$

Thus, relative to the center \(a\), there are only four coordinate shells:

$$
t=0,\qquad
t=p,\qquad
t\ne0\ \text{even},\qquad
t\ne p\ \text{odd}.
$$

Modulo the half-turn kernel \((\epsilon_2,\epsilon_p,\epsilon_{2p})=(-1,1,-1)\), the four distinct sign types give the following four families of cleared-denominator vertices:

1. \(A_i\): value \(N-1\) at \(i\), and \(-1\) elsewhere.
2. \(B_i=-A_i\): value \(-(N-1)\) at \(i\), and \(1\) elsewhere.
3. \(H_i\): value \(N-3\) at \(i\), value \(-3\) on the other coordinates of the same parity as \(i\), and value \(1\) on the opposite parity class.
4. \(L_i=-H_i\): value \(3-N\) at \(i\), value \(3\) on the other coordinates of the same parity as \(i\), and value \(-1\) on the opposite parity class.

Here \(i\) runs over all coordinates.  These \(4N\) vertices are exactly \(N X_{2p}^{\mathrm{spec}}\), since \(M_{2p}=4\).  Any coordinate permutation preserving or swapping the two parity classes sends this list of four families to itself.  Hence

$$
S_p\wr S_2\leq G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_{2p}^{\mathrm{spec}}).
$$

Conversely, the displayed vertex families determine the exact coordinate-pair color.  The lower-bound group is transitive on ordered pairs of distinct coordinates with the same parity, and also on ordered pairs with opposite parity, so there can be at most two nonzero pair colors.  If two coordinates have the same parity, the color contains the ordered value pair \((N-3,-3)\), coming from \(H_i\) with \(i\) equal to the first coordinate.  If two coordinates have opposite parity, no \(H_i\) or \(L_i\) vertex can produce that pair: within a parity class the \(H\)-values are \(N-3\) and \(-3\), while across parity classes the \(H\)-values are paired with \(1\), and the \(L\)-values are paired with \(-1\).  The \(A_i\) and \(B_i\) families do not depend on parity.  Thus the pair colors are exactly the two parity colors, same parity and opposite parity.

By Theorem 8.6, every coordinate stabilizer preserves the induced color fusion.  In this case that means it must preserve or swap the even and odd classes.  Therefore

$$
G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_{2p}^{\mathrm{spec}})
\leq
S_p\wr S_2,
$$

and the two inclusions prove equality. \(\square\)

**Corollary 8.7A: Parity-Only Control for \(N=2p\).**  For every odd prime \(p\), the full spectral compound \(X_{2p}^{\mathrm{spec}}\) and the parity-dual compound \(V_{2p}\cup\rho_2V_{2p}\) have the same coordinate stabilizer:

$$
G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_{2p}^{\mathrm{spec}})
=
G_{\mathrm{coord}}(V_{2p}\cup\rho_2V_{2p})
\cong
S_p\wr S_2.
$$

Thus in the even semiprime family, the smaller parity-only object already detects all coordinate-permutation symmetry of the full block-sign compound.  The additional \(\Phi_p\) and \(\Phi_{2p}\) block signs add vertices and angles, but they do not refine the coordinate symmetry beyond the even/odd bipartition.

This parity dominance is special to \(N=2p\).  Odd prime powers have no parity block, odd squarefree two-prime cases are governed by CRT fibers rather than parity fibers, and mixed composites such as \(N=12,18,20\) exhibit divisor-fusion behavior that is neither purely parity nor purely prime-power p-adic.

The next family is the clean odd squarefree CRT case.

**Theorem 8.8: Odd Squarefree Two-Prime CRT Stabilizers.**  Let \(p<q\) be odd primes and \(N=pq\).  Under the Chinese-remainder identification

$$
\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z
\cong
\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z,
$$

the coordinate-pair colors of \(X_{pq}^{\mathrm{spec}}\) are exactly the three nonzero divisor relations

$$
\gcd(N,h)\in\{1,p,q\}.
$$

Consequently

$$
G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_{pq}^{\mathrm{spec}})
\cong
S_p\times S_q,
$$

acting independently on the two CRT coordinates, of order \(p!q!\).

**Proof.**  The nontrivial divisors of \(N=pq\) are \(p,q,pq\).  Write a coordinate as \((r,s)\in\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z\), and write the center of a block-sign vertex as \((a,b)\).  Put

$$
\alpha=
\begin{cases}
p-1,&r=a,\\
-1,&r\ne a,
\end{cases}
\qquad
\beta=
\begin{cases}
q-1,&s=b,\\
-1,&s\ne b.
\end{cases}
$$

Since \(p\) and \(q\) are coprime, \(c_{pq}(t)=c_p(t)c_q(t)\).  After clearing denominators, every block-sign vertex has coordinate value

$$
N x_{(r,s)}
=
\epsilon_p\alpha+\epsilon_q\beta+\epsilon_{pq}\alpha\beta.
$$

Thus each vertex is constant on the four CRT rectangles determined by the center:

$$
(r=a,s=b),\quad
(r=a,s\ne b),\quad
(r\ne a,s=b),\quad
(r\ne a,s\ne b).
$$

It follows immediately that any independent permutation of the \(p\)-coordinate and the \(q\)-coordinate sends centers to centers and preserves the four-rectangle pattern for every sign choice.  Hence

$$
S_p\times S_q
\leq
G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_{pq}^{\mathrm{spec}}).
$$

This lower-bound group is transitive on each of the three kinds of ordered coordinate pairs: same \(p\)-coordinate and different \(q\)-coordinate, same \(q\)-coordinate and different \(p\)-coordinate, and different in both coordinates.  Thus there are at most three nonzero pair colors.

They are distinct.  In the same \(p\)-coordinate relation, choose two coordinates with common \(p\)-coordinate and choose the center with that same \(p\)-coordinate but with a third \(q\)-coordinate.  With signs

$$
(\epsilon_p,\epsilon_q,\epsilon_{pq})=(1,-1,-1),
$$

both coordinates lie in the shell \((\alpha,\beta)=(p-1,-1)\), so the cleared pair value

$$
(2p-1,2p-1)
$$

occurs.  Inspection of the four shell values shows that the value \(2p-1\) occurs only in the shell \((p-1,-1)\) with this sign pattern.  Therefore this large diagonal pair cannot occur for a pair with different \(p\)-coordinates, because two distinct coordinates can simultaneously lie in the shell \((p-1,-1)\) only when they share the center's \(p\)-coordinate.  Similarly, in the same \(q\)-coordinate relation, the signs

$$
(\epsilon_p,\epsilon_q,\epsilon_{pq})=(-1,1,-1)
$$

give the large diagonal pair

$$
(2q-1,2q-1),
$$

and the value \(2q-1\) occurs only in the shell \((-1,q-1)\) with this sign pattern.  Hence this diagonal pair cannot occur for a pair with different \(q\)-coordinates.  Since \(p<q\), these two diagonal witnesses are different, and the remaining relation is the off-row/off-column relation.  Therefore the pair colors are exactly the three nonzero divisor relations \(g=1,p,q\).

Any coordinate stabilizer preserves these exact pair colors.  Hence it preserves the partition into the \(p\) fibers of the projection to \(\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z\) and the partition into the \(q\) fibers of the projection to \(\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z\).  Intersections of these two fiber systems are singletons, so the permutation must have the form

$$
(r,s)\longmapsto(\sigma(r),\tau(s))
$$

with \(\sigma\in S_p\) and \(\tau\in S_q\).  Thus

$$
G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_{pq}^{\mathrm{spec}})
\leq
S_p\times S_q,
$$

and equality follows. \(\square\)

The remaining evidence suggests the following general reduction.

**Conjecture 8.6A: Pair-Color Sufficiency.**  For every \(N\geq3\), a coordinate permutation \(\pi\in S_N\) preserves \(X_N^{\mathrm{spec}}\) if and only if it preserves the exact coordinate-pair colors:

$$
\mathcal C_N(j-i)=\mathcal C_N(\pi(j)-\pi(i))
\qquad
\text{for all }i,j\in\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z.
$$

Equivalently, \(G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_N^{\mathrm{spec}})\) is the automorphism group of the divisor-scheme fusion determined by the Ramanujan shell profiles \(\mathcal C_N(h)\).

This conjecture is proved above for prime powers through the explicit p-adic shell calculation.  The \(N=12\) case follows from Theorem 8.6, the even semiprime family follows from Theorem 8.7, the odd squarefree two-prime family follows from Theorem 8.8, and the remaining mixed cases displayed in the table are verified by exact pair-color upper bounds and explicit generator lower bounds.

The cases \(N=18\) and \(N=20\) show two different mixed prime-power interactions.  For \(N=18=2\cdot3^2\), the pair colors factor through the divisor scheme on \(\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z\), with a three-point fiber over each residue class modulo \(6\).  This gives

$$
S_3^6\rtimes\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z).
$$

For \(N=20=4\cdot5\), the CRT form

$$
\mathbb Z/20\mathbb Z\cong\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z
$$

keeps the affine divisor scheme on the \(4\)-factor but allows the full \(S_5\) action on the \(5\)-factor:

$$
\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z)\times S_5.
$$

Theorem 8.6 proves that \(N=12\) is the first rigid collapse in this range: the coordinate-pair colors distinguish the full nonzero gcd profile

$$
\gcd(12,i-j)\in\{1,2,3,4,6\},
$$

and the resulting coordinate stabilizer is only the affine cyclic normalizer \(\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z)\).  This is the clearest small example of **divisor-lattice rigidity**: the incompatible \(2^2\)- and \(3\)-layers constrain each other enough to destroy the larger fiber symmetries seen in \(N=6,10,15,18,20\).

Theorem 8.8 shows why the rigidity criterion must be phrased in terms of the full automorphism group of the induced fusion, not just in terms of separating differences up to units.  For \(N=pq\), the pair colors distinguish the full nonzero gcd profile, but the divisor scheme decomposes as a CRT product and has the larger automorphism group \(S_p\times S_q\).

**Conjecture 8.6B: Divisor-Fusion Stabilizer Classification.**  The coordinate stabilizer of \(X_N^{\mathrm{spec}}\) is the automorphism group of the divisor-scheme fusion \(\mathfrak F_N\) induced by the exact pair colors.  Moreover, \(\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak F_N)\) should be computable from the CRT decomposition of \(N\), with complete symmetric groups on indistinguishable fibers and affine factors precisely where the divisor colors leave no additional fiber freedom.

In this formulation, Theorem 8.7 proves all \(N=2p\) parity-fiber collapses, Theorem 8.8 proves the odd two-prime CRT products, \(N=18\) is a blow-up of the divisor scheme on \(\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z\), \(N=20\) has an affine \(\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z\) factor and a full \(S_5\) factor, and \(N=12\) is the first case where the induced fusion has no non-affine freedom.

The arithmetic variation in these stabilizer tables is the main warning against overgeneralizing from prime powers.  The affine cyclic normalizer

$$
\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)
=
C_N\rtimes(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^\times
$$

centralizes the block-sign group and therefore gives a safe spectral-affine subgroup of the orthogonal stabilizer:

$$
\frac{B_N\times \operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)}{K_N}
\leq
O(P_N^{\mathrm{spec}}),
$$

of order

$$
M_N\,N\,\varphi(N).
$$

This is an orthogonal-stabilizer lower bound, not a coordinate-stabilizer count.  It may therefore be larger than the coordinate stabilizer columns above; the extra symmetries are block-sign operators rather than coordinate permutations.

This subgroup is not generally the full stabilizer.  In prime dimensions the full stabilizer expands to \(C_2\times S_p\).  In prime-power dimensions, Theorem 8.5 explains the imprimitive p-adic wreath products visible at \(N=8\) and \(N=9\).  In mixed composite dimensions the coordinate stabilizer can still be much larger than the affine cyclic normalizer, as Theorem 8.7 shows for \(N=2p\), or collapse back to it, as proved for \(N=12\).  The full Gram/orthogonal stabilizer of \(P_N^{\mathrm{spec}}\) is therefore left as a separate classification problem rather than asserted from the current data.

The theorem and conjectural tables also draw a boundary around what is not yet
proved.  The divisor coordinate colors always sit inside a Schurian association
scheme, but the projective Ramanujan angle relations of Theorem 8.2 need not
automatically be an association scheme after fusion.  Likewise, the tight-frame
statement by itself does not imply spherical-design, fusion-frame, SIC, or
Grassmannian optimality.  Those refinements belong to the next round of the
program.

### 8.9 The \(N=8\) Fourier-Plane Test

The next three subsections are not needed for the construction of the parity-dual simplex, the prism/free-sum hull, or the divisor-scheme stabilizer theorems.  They serve a different purpose: they close off the naive route from cyclotomic block signs to a Shephard-Todd classification.  The outcome is an obstruction, not a new irreducible reflection group.  The arithmetic normalizers sit over products of rank-two imprimitive factors on conjugate Fourier pairs, while the Galois permutations that couple those factors are normalizer symmetries rather than complex reflections.

The first higher-block test for the complex-reflection bridge is \(N=8\).  Let

$$
E_8=H_8^{[8]}\otimes\mathbb C
=
\mathbb C\chi_1\oplus\mathbb C\chi_3\oplus
\mathbb C\chi_5\oplus\mathbb C\chi_7,
$$

and put \(\zeta=\zeta_8\).  On this ordered basis, the cyclic shift and Galois relabelings act by

$$
C_8=\operatorname{diag}(\zeta,\zeta^3,\zeta^5,\zeta^7),
\qquad
P_u\chi_k=\chi_{uk},
\quad u\in(\mathbb Z/8\mathbb Z)^\times.
$$

The real Fourier planes are the conjugate pairs

$$
E_{17}=\mathbb C\chi_1\oplus\mathbb C\chi_7,
\qquad
E_{35}=\mathbb C\chi_3\oplus\mathbb C\chi_5.
$$

**Theorem 8.9: The \(N=8\) Reflection-Group Obstruction.**  The arithmetic normalizer

$$
A_8=\langle C_8,\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\zeta_8)/\mathbb Q)\rangle
$$

acting on \(E_8\) contains no complex reflections.  Refining the real \(\Phi_8\) block into independent signs on the two conjugate Fourier planes still gives no complex reflections.  The maximal reflection-generated group obtained from the conjugate-pair Fourier refinement is

$$
G(8,8,2)\times G(8,8,2),
$$

acting on \(E_{17}\oplus E_{35}\).  The Galois unit \(3\) exchanges the two conjugate pairs and extends this product to

$$
\bigl(G(8,8,2)\times G(8,8,2)\bigr)\rtimes C_2^{\mathrm{Gal}},
$$

of order \(512\), but the extension is not reflection-generated; its reflection subgroup is the reducible product \(G(8,8,2)\times G(8,8,2)\) of order \(256\).

The exact monomial calculation is summarized in the following table.  Here "reflection subgroup" means the subgroup generated by all elements whose fixed space in \(E_8\) has complex codimension one.  The real plane-sign refinement allows independent signs on \(E_{17}\) and \(E_{35}\).  The complex sign-reflection refinement allows independent signs on the four character lines \(\mathbb C\chi_k\), together with \(C_8\) and the Galois \(V_4\)-action.  The complex phase-reflection refinement allows each character line to be independently multiplied by an eighth root of unity, again with the Galois \(V_4\)-action permuting the lines.

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
\text{group}&\text{order}&\text{reflections}&\text{reflection subgroup order}\\
\hline
\langle C_8,\operatorname{Gal}\rangle&32&0&1\\
\text{real plane-sign refinement}&64&0&1\\
\text{complex sign-reflection refinement}&256&4&16\\
\text{complex phase-reflection refinement}&16384&28&4096\\
G(8,8,2)\times G(8,8,2)&256&16&256\\
\bigl(G(8,8,2)\times G(8,8,2)\bigr)\rtimes C_2^{\mathrm{Gal}}&512&16&256
\end{array}
$$

**Proof.**  A monomial operator on the basis \(\chi_1,\chi_3,\chi_5,\chi_7\) is encoded by a permutation \(\pi\) of the four labels and exponents \(a_k\in\mathbb Z/8\mathbb Z\), meaning

$$
\chi_k\longmapsto \zeta^{a_k}\chi_{\pi(k)}.
$$

The fixed-space dimension is the number of cycles of \(\pi\) whose total exponent is \(0\pmod 8\).  Therefore a complex reflection is exactly a nonidentity monomial element with fixed-space dimension \(3\).

The arithmetic normalizer has order \(8\cdot4=32\).  Its nontrivial permutation parts are the three double transpositions coming from the nontrivial units modulo \(8\), and its nontrivial diagonal parts move all four primitive character lines by powers of \(C_8\).  No element has fixed-space dimension \(3\).  Adding independent real plane signs changes only pairs of character lines, so it still cannot produce codimension-one fixed space.

On \(E_{17}\), the restriction of \(C_8\) is

$$
\operatorname{diag}(\zeta,\zeta^{-1}),
$$

and the Galois unit \(-1\) swaps \(\chi_1\) and \(\chi_7\).  These two matrices generate the rank-two dihedral complex reflection group \(G(8,8,2)\), of order \(16\).  The same construction on \(E_{35}\) gives a second independent copy.  Thus their product is reflection-generated, has order \(16^2=256\), and has \(16\) codimension-one reflections, eight from each factor.

The remaining Galois unit \(3\) exchanges the two conjugate pairs by two simultaneous transpositions.  This normalizes the product but is not a reflection.  Since every element outside the product has a nontrivial cross-pair permutation part, the fixed-space cycle count is never \(3\).  Hence the reflection subgroup of the order-\(512\) normalizer extension is exactly \(G(8,8,2)\times G(8,8,2)\).  The two intermediate sign and phase rows follow from the same cycle-sum criterion: diagonal coordinate signs give four reflections and generate only the \(2^4\) diagonal sign group, while diagonal eighth-root phases give \(4(8-1)=28\) coordinate reflections and generate the \((\mathbb Z/8)^4\) diagonal subgroup, not the Galois permutation part. \(\square\)

Thus the natural arithmetic normalizer \(\langle C_8,\operatorname{Gal}\rangle\) contains no complex reflections.  Refining \(\rho_8\) into independent real plane signs still gives no complex reflections, because each plane sign has complex fixed space of codimension two in \(E_8\).  If one allows coordinate hyperplane reflections \(\chi_k\mapsto-\chi_k\), the diagonal reflection subgroup appears, but the Galois \(V_4\) permutation part is still not generated by reflections: its nontrivial elements act by double transpositions on the primitive labels, not by single transpositions.

The positive reflection-group object is rank two and reducible.  On each conjugate pair \(E_{17}\) and \(E_{35}\), the rotation

$$
\operatorname{diag}(\zeta,\zeta^{-1})
$$

together with conjugation generates the dihedral complex reflection group \(G(8,8,2)\) of order \(16\).  Their independent product is the reducible reflection group \(G(8,8,2)\times G(8,8,2)\).  The remaining Galois unit \(3\) exchanges the two conjugate pairs by two simultaneous transpositions; this normalizes the product but is not itself a reflection, and the resulting order-\(512\) extension is not reflection-generated.

The \(N=8\) test therefore gives a concrete obstruction rather than a new irreducible Shephard-Todd example.  The natural arithmetic object lives in a normalizer of a reducible imprimitive reflection arrangement.  If the phase-reflection refinement were also enlarged by replacing the Galois \(V_4\) permutation image with the full \(S_4\), one would obtain the imprimitive reflection group \(G(8,1,4)\), but that enlargement no longer comes from the cyclic/Galois spectral data.  The next natural ambient theories are therefore finite monomial normalizers and imprimitive unitary reflection groups [Blichfeldt; Read; Hughes], together with possible Heisenberg-Weil normalizers of the cyclic shift action.

### 8.10 The \(N=9\) Follow-Up

The next test checks whether the obstruction is only a parity artifact of \(N=8\).  For \(N=9\),

$$
E_9=H_9^{[9]}\otimes\mathbb C
=
\bigoplus_{k\in(\mathbb Z/9\mathbb Z)^\times}\mathbb C\chi_k
$$

has six primitive character lines and three conjugate Fourier pairs:

$$
\{\chi_1,\chi_8\},\qquad
\{\chi_2,\chi_7\},\qquad
\{\chi_4,\chi_5\}.
$$

The Galois group \((\mathbb Z/9\mathbb Z)^\times\cong C_6\) acts cyclically on these three pairs after quotienting by conjugation.  Thus \(N=9\) is the first case where a genuine cross-pair \(3\)-cycle could have broken the \(N=8\) pattern.

**Theorem 8.10: The \(N=9\) Reflection-Group Obstruction.**  The arithmetic normalizer

$$
A_9=\langle C_9,\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\zeta_9)/\mathbb Q)\rangle
$$

acting on \(E_9\) contains no complex reflections.  Refining the real \(\Phi_9\) block into independent signs on the three conjugate Fourier planes still gives no complex reflections.  The maximal reflection-generated group obtained from the conjugate-pair Fourier refinement is

$$
G(9,9,2)^3,
$$

one factor for each conjugate pair.  The cross-pair Galois \(3\)-cycle extends this product to

$$
G(9,9,2)^3\rtimes C_3^{\mathrm{Gal}},
$$

of order \(17496\), but the extension is not reflection-generated; its reflection subgroup is \(G(9,9,2)^3\), of order \(5832\).

The exact monomial calculation is summarized by:

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
\text{group}&\text{order}&\text{reflections}&\text{reflection subgroup order}\\
\hline
\langle C_9,\operatorname{Gal}\rangle&54&0&1\\
\text{real plane-sign refinement}&432&0&1\\
G(9,9,2)^3&5832&27&5832\\
G(9,9,2)^3\rtimes C_3^{\mathrm{Gal}}&17496&27&5832
\end{array}
$$

**Proof.**  Use the same monomial encoding and cycle-sum criterion as in Theorem 8.9, now with exponents modulo \(9\) on the primitive labels \(1,2,4,5,7,8\).  A complex reflection on the six-dimensional space \(E_9\) must have fixed-space dimension \(5\).

The arithmetic normalizer has order \(9\varphi(9)=54\).  The identity permutation with a nontrivial power of \(C_9\) fixes no primitive character line.  The conjugation unit \(-1\) has the three two-cycles \((1,8),(2,7),(4,5)\), and each cycle has total exponent \(0\pmod 9\), so its fixed-space dimension is \(3\), not \(5\).  Units of order \(3\) have two three-cycles, and units of order \(6\) have one six-cycle; the cycle-sum criterion gives fixed-space dimension at most \(2\) in the former case and at most \(1\) in the latter.  Hence \(A_9\) contains no complex reflections.

Adding independent real signs on the three conjugate Fourier planes still acts on whole two-dimensional pair blocks, so any nontrivial such sign has fixed space of codimension at least two.  Therefore the real plane-sign refinement also contains no complex reflections.

On each conjugate pair \(\mathbb C\chi_k\oplus\mathbb C\chi_{-k}\), the restriction of \(C_9\) is

$$
\operatorname{diag}(\zeta_9^k,\zeta_9^{-k}),
$$

and conjugation swaps the two lines.  These generate \(G(9,9,2)\), of order \(18\), on that pair.  The three pair groups commute on the direct-sum decomposition, so their product has order \(18^3=5832\) and is reflection-generated.  The Galois action cycles the three conjugate pairs and normalizes this product.  Its nontrivial cross-pair elements permute two-dimensional summands in a three-cycle, whose fixed space is the two-dimensional diagonal across the three summands, hence has codimension \(4\), not codimension \(1\).  Thus the order-\(17496\) normalizer extension has reflection subgroup exactly \(G(9,9,2)^3\). \(\square\)

Thus the \(N=9\) test supports the stronger conjectural direction: the obstruction is not merely a parity-of-Galois-permutation effect at \(N=8\).  The natural refined objects appear to be reducible products of rank-two cyclotomic dihedral reflection groups, extended by non-reflection Galois normalizers.  In this reading, the simplicial-cyclotomic program meets Shephard-Todd theory through imprimitive factors, but its native ambient category is the monomial normalizer of the cyclotomic Fourier decomposition.

**Conjecture 8.10A: Cyclotomic Monomial Normalizer Hypothesis.**  Let \(d\mid N\) with \(\varphi(d)\geq4\), and let

$$
E_d=H_N^{[d]}\otimes\mathbb C
=
\bigoplus_{k\in(\mathbb Z/d\mathbb Z)^\times}\mathbb C\chi_k.
$$

Then the arithmetic normalizer

$$
\langle C_N,\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\zeta_d)/\mathbb Q)\rangle
$$

acting on \(E_d\) contains no complex reflections.  The maximal reflection-generated subgroup of the natural Fourier-plane refinement is the direct product of

$$
\frac{\varphi(d)}{2}
$$

rank-two dihedral complex reflection groups \(G(d,d,2)\), one for each conjugate Fourier pair \(\{\chi_k,\chi_{-k}\}\).  The cross-pair Galois action normalizes this product but is not itself generated by complex reflections.

Theorems 8.9 and 8.10 prove this hypothesis for \(d=8\) and \(d=9\).  The next genuinely mixed test is \(N=12\), where the \(\Phi_2,\Phi_3,\Phi_4,\Phi_6,\Phi_{12}\) blocks coexist and the coordinate-pair rigidity theorem has already shown that divisor layers can constrain one another.

### 8.11 The \(N=12\) Multi-Block Test

For \(N=12\), the complexified zero-sum space has nonzero Fourier labels

$$
1,2,\ldots,11,
$$

split into cyclotomic blocks

$$
\Phi_2:\{6\},\quad
\Phi_3:\{4,8\},\quad
\Phi_4:\{3,9\},\quad
\Phi_6:\{2,10\},\quad
\Phi_{12}:\{1,5,7,11\}.
$$

The \(\Phi_{12}\) block has two conjugate Fourier pairs, \(\{1,11\}\) and \(\{5,7\}\).  The other nontrivial two-dimensional blocks each have one conjugate pair.

**Theorem 8.11: The \(N=12\) Multi-Block Refinement.**  The arithmetic normalizer

$$
A_{12}=\langle C_{12},\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\zeta_{12})/\mathbb Q)\rangle
$$

acting on \(H_{12}\otimes\mathbb C\) contains no complex reflections.  The reflection-generated Fourier-plane refinement decomposes blockwise as

$$
R_{12}
\cong
C_2
\times G(3,3,2)
\times G(4,4,2)
\times G(6,6,2)
\times G(12,12,2)^2.
$$

It has order

$$
|R_{12}|=
2\cdot6\cdot8\cdot12\cdot24^2
=663552
$$

and has

$$
1+3+4+6+12+12=38
$$

codimension-one complex reflections.  The only extra Galois normalizer action not already contained in \(R_{12}\) exchanges the two \(\Phi_{12}\) pair factors, giving

$$
\widetilde R_{12}
\cong
R_{12}\rtimes C_2^{\mathrm{Gal}},
\qquad
|\widetilde R_{12}|=1327104.
$$

This extension is not reflection-generated, and its reflection subgroup is exactly \(R_{12}\).

**Proof.**  The monomial cycle-sum criterion from Theorem 8.9 applies to the eleven primitive nonzero Fourier labels.  For an arithmetic element \(C_{12}^aP_u\), with \(u\in(\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z)^\times=\{1,5,7,11\}\), a complex reflection would require fixed-space dimension \(10\).  The cycle-sum profiles are:

$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
u&\text{possible fixed-space dimensions of }C_{12}^aP_u\\
\hline
1&0,1,2,3,5,11\\
5&2,5,7\\
7&1,2,5,8\\
11&5,6.
\end{array}
$$

Thus no nonidentity arithmetic element has fixed-space dimension \(10\), and \(A_{12}\) contains no complex reflections.

The \(\Phi_2\) block is the single line \(\mathbb C\chi_6\), so changing its sign is a codimension-one reflection on \(H_{12}\otimes\mathbb C\).  On each conjugate pair \(\{\chi_k,\chi_{-k}\}\), the restriction of \(C_{12}\) gives

$$
\operatorname{diag}(\zeta_{12}^k,\zeta_{12}^{-k}),
$$

whose order is \(d=12/\gcd(12,k)\).  Together with conjugation on that pair, it generates \(G(d,d,2)\), of order \(2d\), with \(d\) reflections.  Applying this to the pairs

$$
\{4,8\},\quad\{3,9\},\quad\{2,10\},\quad\{1,11\},\quad\{5,7\}
$$

gives the five factors \(G(3,3,2)\), \(G(4,4,2)\), \(G(6,6,2)\), \(G(12,12,2)\), and \(G(12,12,2)\).  These act on mutually orthogonal Fourier summands, so their product with the \(\Phi_2\) sign factor is reflection-generated, has the displayed order, and has the displayed reflection count.

Finally, the global Galois group preserves every \(\Phi_d\) block.  Its restrictions to the \(\Phi_3,\Phi_4,\Phi_6\) pair blocks are already contained in the corresponding rank-two factors, and its action on the \(\Phi_2\) line is trivial.  The only remaining action is the exchange of the two conjugate-pair factors inside \(\Phi_{12}\).  This doubles the product order.  Any element involving that exchange swaps two two-dimensional summands, so its fixed space on the exchanged four-dimensional part has dimension at most two; hence it has codimension at least two there and cannot be a complex reflection on the full space.  Therefore the reflection subgroup of \(\widetilde R_{12}\) is exactly \(R_{12}\). \(\square\)

The \(N=12\) result closes the first multi-block case.  The coordinate-level divisor rigidity from Theorem 8.6 does not force cross-block coupling in the Fourier-plane reflection refinement: the reflection-generated part is the independent blockwise product predicted by Conjecture 8.10A, and the only extra normalizer action is internal to the \(\Phi_{12}\) block.

**Conjecture 8.11A: Multi-Block Reflection Product.**  For arbitrary \(N\), let \(R_N\) be the maximal reflection-generated subgroup of the natural Fourier-plane refinement of the cyclotomic block-sign action on

$$
H_N\otimes\mathbb C.
$$

Then \(R_N\) decomposes as the direct product of the conjugate-pair factors

$$
R_N
\cong
C_2^{\epsilon_2(N)}
\times
\prod_{\substack{d\mid N\\ d>2}}
\prod_{\{k,-k\}\subset(\mathbb Z/d\mathbb Z)^\times}
G(d,d,2).
$$

Here \(\epsilon_2(N)=1\) if \(2\mid N\) and \(\epsilon_2(N)=0\) otherwise; the \(C_2\) factor is the sign reflection on the \(\Phi_2\) line, when that line exists, and the inner product ranges over conjugate primitive Fourier pairs in the \(\Phi_d\) block.  Equivalently, each real cyclotomic block contributes independently: a one-dimensional sign factor for \(d=2\), and one rank-two imprimitive dihedral complex reflection factor for each conjugate Fourier plane when \(d>2\).

The full arithmetic normalizer may contain additional Galois actions that permute conjugate-pair factors inside a fixed \(\Phi_d\) block.  The conjecture is that such cross-pair normalizer actions are not generated by complex reflections and that no divisor-lattice coupling between distinct \(\Phi_d\) blocks contributes new reflections.  Conjecture 8.10A is the single-block case with \(\varphi(d)\geq4\); Theorems 8.9, 8.10, and 8.11 verify the product statement for \(N=8,9,12\).

**Observation 8.12: Layer Separation and the \(\Phi_2\) Meeting Point.**  The results above separate two rigidity layers that are easy to conflate.

At the coordinate-permutation layer, the relevant invariant is the divisor-scheme fusion \(\mathfrak F_N\) on \(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z\) induced by exact coordinate-pair colors.  This layer can couple divisor data across prime-power components: Theorem 8.6 gives

$$
G_{\mathrm{coord}}(X_{12}^{\mathrm{spec}})
=
\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z),
$$

so the mixed divisor lattice for \(N=12\) leaves no non-affine coordinate-fiber freedom.  Theorems 8.5, 8.7, and 8.8 show different coordinate-layer outcomes in the prime-power, even semiprime, and odd squarefree two-prime families, and Conjecture 8.6B formulates the expected general divisor-fusion classification.

At the Fourier-plane reflection layer, the invariant is instead the conjugate-pair decomposition inside each cyclotomic block.  In the proved cases \(N=8,9,12\), the maximal reflection-generated subgroup is the direct product of the corresponding rank-two \(G(d,d,2)\) factors, together with the \(\Phi_2\) sign factor when present.  The \(N=12\) theorem is the decisive mixed test: the coordinate-level divisor rigidity from Theorem 8.6 does not propagate into cross-block reflection coupling.

The only nontrivial block where the two layers visibly meet is \(\Phi_2\).  The cyclotomic polynomials with \(\varphi(d)=1\) are only \(\Phi_1\) and \(\Phi_2\).  The \(\Phi_1\) line is the constant line and is removed by passing to the zero-sum space \(H_N\).  Thus, inside \(H_N\), the \(\Phi_2\) line is the unique one-dimensional cyclotomic component.  When \(N\) is even it is spanned by

$$
s_i=(-1)^i,
$$

whose two level sets are exactly the parity classes in the coordinate index set.  The same rank-one projector \(P_2\) therefore appears in both languages: coordinate permutations see the parity bipartition, while Fourier-plane refinements see the \(C_2\) sign reflection \(\rho_2=I-2P_2\).  For every \(d>2\), the real block has dimension at least two and the reflection layer splits into conjugate Fourier planes rather than a coordinate bipartition.  In this precise sense, \(\Phi_2\) is the unique nontrivial meeting point of the coordinate and reflection layers.

This leaves the following concrete open problems.

1. **Mixed-composite structural theorem.**  Classify the multi-simplex compound

$$
X_N^{\mathrm{spec}}.
$$

Beyond the polar-intersection formula, the prime-family theorem, the prime-power coordinate-stabilizer theorem, the even semiprime parity-collapse theorem, the odd two-prime CRT theorem, and the \(N=12\) rigidity theorem above, compute its full face lattice, full orthogonal stabilizer, and possible product/free-sum descriptions for composite families such as higher squarefree and mixed prime-power \(N\).  The desired theorem should explain when the divisor-lattice colors collapse to parity fibers, CRT product factors, p-adic fibers, or the affine cyclic normalizer.

2. **Association schemes and frame applications.**  Determine when the projective Ramanujan angle relations form an association scheme, spherical design, fusion frame, or related coherent configuration.  Theorem 8.6 supplies the coordinate-level divisor scheme; the remaining question is which angle fusions are closed enough to support coding-theoretic, periodic signal detection, or sparse-recovery applications.

3. **Fourier-plane refinements.**  For \(\varphi(d)>2\), the real block \(H_N^{[d]}\) contains several conjugate Fourier planes.  One can refine the block-sign involution into plane flips, conjugations, and Galois permutations of these planes.  The resulting group is no longer just \((C_2)^r\); it should be compared with signed permutation groups, Weyl groups, and normalizers of cyclotomic Fourier decompositions.

4. **Complex reflection comparison.**  The refined Fourier-plane picture is adjacent to the theory of finite unitary reflection groups [Shephard-Todd; Broue-Malle-Rouquier; Lehrer-Taylor], but Sections 8.9 and 8.10 show why it should not be identified with a Shephard-Todd group too quickly.  The \(N=8\) and \(N=9\) arithmetic normalizers fragment into normalizer extensions of products of \(G(d,d,2)\)-type factors, not irreducible reflection groups.  The next problem is to determine whether higher \(\Phi_d\) blocks follow the same pattern: reducible imprimitive reflection factors on conjugate Fourier pairs, extended by non-reflection Galois normalizers.

5. **Higher multi-block tests.**  Theorem 8.11 shows that \(N=12\) is multiplicative at the Fourier-plane reflection level, despite being rigid at the coordinate-permutation level.  Test Conjecture 8.11A in higher mixed cases such as \(N=18,20,24\), where several higher-dimensional \(\Phi_d\) blocks coexist, and determine whether the blockwise product behavior persists throughout the monomial normalizer.

These problems go beyond the tight-frame theorem above.  The point of the \(\Phi_2\) case is that it is the one block where the reflection is one-dimensional, canonical, visible as a coordinate bipartition, and already explains the stella-octangula anomaly; the full \(B_N\)-orbit shows that the same spectral language also generates a broader arithmetic family of few-angle tight frames.

## 9. Conclusion

The parity-dual simplex theorem gives the even-\(N\) generalization of the canonical-involution part of the \(N=4\) stella-octangula intuition.  The construction is canonical because it is spectral: it uses the \(\Phi_2\) component of the cyclic shift.

For \(N=4\), the parity-dual tetrahedron is exactly the complementary tetrahedron of the stella octangula, up to cyclic relabeling, and \(\operatorname{conv}(X_4)\) is the cube.  The identity \(-I_{H_4}=C_4^2\rho_2\) also recovers the original Janus orientation bit: the parity reflection is the operation that reverses the \(H_4\) volume form, while the cyclic half-turn is orientation-preserving.  The Boolean Janus theorem records the arithmetic lift of this bit: for \(R\cong\mathbb F_2^k\), the orientation quotient is \(B_R/K_R\) with dimension \(2^k-1-k\).  For larger even \(N\), the same reflection produces a second regular simplex ETF whose cross-Gram matrix has a three-valued parity pattern, but it is no longer the antipodal simplex.  The stabilizer theorem gives the parity-class wreath product for \(N\geq6\), and the small cases in Appendix A verify its first instances.  Polyhedrally, the compound is the vertex set of a prism over the free sum of two equal simplices.  Frame-theoretically, it is best viewed as a structured unit-norm tight frame made from a pair of simplex ETFs rather than as a single optimal equiangular frame.  For odd \(N\), no such cyclic parity dual exists.

Replacing the single parity reflection by the full spectral family gives the first broader payoff: the block-sign orbit \(X_N^{\mathrm{spec}}\) is a centrally symmetric unit-norm tight frame whose non-antipodal projective angles are controlled exactly by Ramanujan sums.  Its Gram matrix is block-circulant with circulant blocks, and its entries are cyclotomic traces.  The packing table shows the honest status of this construction: prime cases are Welch-optimal antipodal simplices, but the small composite cases are not close to the Welch or Levenshtein line-packing baselines.  Their strength is arithmetic structure, not Grassmannian optimality.  The convex hull is dual to an intersection of block-signed central cube slices, and in prime dimensions it is exactly the antipodal-simplex hull \(\operatorname{conv}(V_p\cup -V_p)\) with polar \(Q_p=[-1,1]^p\cap H_p\).

The composite theory shows that the next layer is genuinely arithmetic: already \(N=6\) has a new 24-vertex hull with f-vector \((24,108,192,144,38)\), the conjectural rows \(N=8,9,10\) have distinct larger face lattices, prime-power coordinate stabilizers are governed by p-adic congruence-tree wreath products with a special top-layer fusion for powers of two, coordinate-pair colors are fusions of the divisor association scheme, all even semiprimes \(N=2p\) have parity-fiber stabilizer \(S_p\wr S_2\) and no larger coordinate symmetry than the parity-only compound, all odd squarefree two-prime cases \(N=pq\) have CRT stabilizer \(S_p\times S_q\), and the mixed case \(N=12\) has coordinate stabilizer exactly \(\operatorname{Aff}(\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z)\).  The \(N=8\), \(N=9\), and \(N=12\) Fourier-plane tests give concrete reflection-theoretic obstructions: the natural arithmetic refinements normalize products of rank-two \(G(d,d,2)\)-type reflection groups but are not themselves generated by complex reflections, and the first multi-block case remains blockwise multiplicative.  Thus the coordinate-permutation layer and Fourier-plane reflection layer are governed by different invariants, meeting only at the \(\Phi_2\) line: the unique nonconstant one-dimensional cyclotomic component of \(H_N\), visible both as the parity bipartition and as the parity sign reflection.  Squarefree and mixed-composite face-lattice classifications, full orthogonal stabilizers, higher cyclotomic monomial normalizers, and possible Heisenberg-Weil interpretations remain the next layer of the simplicial-cyclotomic program.

## Appendix A. Computational Methodology

The computations are supplementary checks and finite censuses.  They are not prerequisites for the formal theorems except where explicitly cited as conjectural evidence.

The parity-dual verifier

$$
\texttt{empirical/verify\_parity\_dual\_simplices.py}
$$

checks the parity vector, reflection identities, regular-simplex Gram identities, the cross-Gram formula of Theorem 3.1, the \(N=4\) specialization, the absence of a \(-1\) cyclic eigenvalue for odd \(N\), and the first \(N=6\) stabilizer case.

The parity-dual census scripts

$$
\texttt{empirical/census\_parity\_dual\_simplices.py},
\qquad
\texttt{empirical/census\_parity\_dual\_polytope.py}
$$

compute exact Gram profiles, coherence comparisons, small coordinate stabilizers, and f-vectors obtained from the exact prism/free-sum decomposition.  Their reports are

$$
\texttt{empirical/reports/parity\_dual\_census.md},
\qquad
\texttt{empirical/reports/parity\_dual\_polytope.md}.
$$

The block-sign frame census

$$
\texttt{empirical/census\_cyclotomic\_block\_signs.py}
$$

checks Proposition 8.1 for \(3\leq N\leq16\), computes the number of distinct simplex images in the full \(B_N\)-orbit, verifies the frame operator in Theorem 8.2, records the projective angle profiles, and produces the Welch/Levenshtein packing-baseline table used in Section 8.2.1.  Its report is

$$
\texttt{empirical/reports/cyclotomic\_block\_signs.md}.
$$

The block-sign polytope census

$$
\texttt{empirical/census\_cyclotomic\_block\_polytope.py}
$$

checks the prime f-vector formula from Theorem 8.3, performs exact facet enumeration for \(N=6\), and computes small coordinate-stabilizer data using coordinate-pair color upper bounds.  For \(N=8,9,10\), Qhull/SciPy facet hyperplanes are used only as numerical seeds; every exposed facet is reconstructed and checked in exact rational arithmetic before face numbers are counted.  Its report is

$$
\texttt{empirical/reports/cyclotomic\_block\_polytope.md}.
$$

\texttt{empirical/verify\_prime\_power\_stabilizers.py}

checks the p-adic congruence-tree stabilizer theorem for \(N=8,9,16,25,27,32\), including coordinate-pair color partitions and generators for the predicted stabilizer groups.  Its report is

\texttt{empirical/reports/prime\_power\_stabilizers.md}.
$$

The mixed-composite stabilizer verifier

$$
\texttt{empirical/verify\_mixed\_composite\_stabilizers.py}

checks coordinate-pair color upper bounds and generator lower bounds for \(N=6,10,12,15,18,20\).  Its report is

$$
\texttt{empirical/reports/mixed\_composite\_stabilizers.md}.
$$

\texttt{empirical/analyze\_phi8\_refined\_group.py}

uses exact monomial matrices on the primitive Fourier labels \(\{1,3,5,7\}\) to compute the arithmetic normalizer, real plane-sign refinements, complex coordinate-reflection refinements, and the reducible \(G(8,8,2)\times G(8,8,2)\) pair product discussed in Section 8.9.  Its report is
